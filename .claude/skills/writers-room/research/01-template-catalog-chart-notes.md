## 3 Act Feature Story Template (xlsx), Sheet1 comments, paraphrased

Author of the template text is not named in the file. The structure (Orphan, Wanderer, Warrior, Martyr; 12 Act I plot points; yes/no reversal pairs) matches Carol Pearson's hero archetypes as adapted for screenwriting. Attribution not verified; treat the template as an anonymous source.

### Act I: 12 plot points
1. Open on the hero (in the ordinary world), the victim/stakes character (at or just before victimization), or the antagonist. Often antagonist and victim together.
2. Show the hero's flaw through the Stakes Character. The Stakes Character is the human face of everyone the antagonist harms, usually close to the hero, and usually has the quality the hero lacks.
3. Meet the antagonist, or a representative, or an act that shows what the antagonist wants.
4. The Deflector pulls the hero off his path (misguided friend or bad guy; can be several).
5. Inciting event: the hero becomes emotionally involved (or the audience does on his behalf).
6. The goal is clarified in relation to the Stakes Character or love interest; the hero's problem becomes clear to the audience. Notes the cliche "Do you know what your problem is?" line as overused but the intent as correct.
7. An Ally pushes the hero out of the status quo. Want and need are different; the hero often gets the want only after the need.
8. The hero hesitates (Refusal of the Call).
9. Conflict with the Antagonist or a Deflector stops the hero or threatens the emotional stakes; the background threat moves to the foreground.
10. Stakes made absolutely clear: depth of feeling for the Stakes Character, or severity of threat to victims.
11. Deflector or Antagonist threatens to take the Stakes Character away (hero may not know yet).
12. Hero decides to act. End of Act I: the Central Question is fully clear.

### Act II: 14 yes/no reversal pairs
- Pairs 1 to 3: allies and mentor; villain shown as really bad (or opposition as very strong); hero pursues what he thinks he needs while glimpsing what he really needs, and denies it; low-level opposition as dry runs; secondary/love story begins.
- Pairs 4 to 5: hero still wandering, learning, testing abilities; obstacles intensify; opposition becomes aware of the hero.
- Pairs 6 to 7: threat to the Stakes Character reaches life or death (literal or figurative); the third-act solution is shown, its meaning missed; the opposite value of the theme is stated; hero knows the rules; villain's plan explained; hero enters the villain's world.
- Pairs 8 to 10 (second half): hero shifts to active, direct pursuit; initial success; opposition tightens; thematic argument raised with pro and con voiced by different characters; hero bounces between; prepares unknowingly for change.
- Pairs 11 to 12: intense direct conflict; hero realizes he lacks the skill; darkest internal space, often a tight enclosed place; Ultimate Test, a death-moment.
- Pairs 13 to 14: resurrection with new understanding; the worst that can happen happens; villain gains upper hand; hero becomes standard bearer of the positive theme; lowest moment, possibly a character interlude.

### Act III
- Big Yes / No: hero commits to change (resolves Private); accepts sacrifice for the Stakes Character (sets up Personal); does the bravest thing (sets up Professional); fails, presses on.
- Big No / Final Yes: Central Question nearly answered negatively; huge sacrifice; earlier-planted third-act solution becomes essential; Private resolves, enabling Personal, enabling Professional (order flexible; closer together is more satisfying; all three at once is best); short denouement, hero returned to ordinary world embodying the positive theme ("The Hollywood Ending").

### Central Question
The pivotal question; once answered yes or no, the film ends. Three layers of conflict: Professional (visible to everyone), Personal (shared with a few), Private (faced alone).

### The Formula
"When a TYPE OF PERSON has/does/wants/gets A, he gets/does/tries/learns B, only to discover that C now happens; and he must respond by doing D." A = Orphan, B = Wanderer, C = Warrior, D = Martyr. 60 to 80 words, the full scope of the story, a promise to the audience.

### Archetypes (Sheet2 comments)
- Orphan (Act I): literally or figuratively alone; imposed or chosen (duty, rebellion, selfishness, reserve); unique from the world around him.
- Wanderer (Act II-A): searches for the answer to the Central Question; clues, helpers, opponents, skills.
- Warrior (Act II-B): from midpoint has most of what he needs or has run out of time; bloodied, has reserves; near end of Act II goes to his darkest place and dies literally or figuratively, often in an enclosed space; reborn with the answer.
- Martyr (Act III): must be willing to lose everything; no longer driven by success but by what is right or necessary; then gets the success. The Martyr can be another character whose example teaches the hero.

### Four questions (Sheet2 comments)
1. Hero MUST be sympathetic: undeserved misfortune, or "petting the dog".
2. Goal must be compelling: if he does not do it, it will not get done, and innocent people will suffer too.
3. Main opponent ruthlessly committed to a mutually exclusive goal; only one winner.
4. Stakes of failure: "there can be only one answer: death." Real physical death when possible, otherwise a figurative death "so strong it might as well be real death."
