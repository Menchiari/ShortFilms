# Web research notes: writers, directors, Catmull, AI tells

Collected 2026-11-10. Verification labels used everywhere in the kit:
- [verified: primary] the words appear in a primary source I read (transcript, the person's own interview text, the book).
- [verified: secondary] the words are reproduced by a reputable secondary source that quotes the primary (I did not see the original page).
- [unverified] attribution or exact wording not confirmed. Do not quote these as fact.

## Stephen King, On Writing (2000)
- "Write with the door closed, rewrite with the door open." [verified: secondary, signalvnoise.com excerpts of the book; also quoted in willpatrick.co.uk notes]
- Rejection-slip note he received: "Not bad, but PUFFY. You need to revise for length. Formula: 2nd Draft = 1st Draft - 10%. Good luck." [verified: secondary, signalvnoise]
- On pacing: he goes back to Elmore Leonard, who "just left out the boring parts." Cutting speeds pace: "(kill your darlings, kill your darlings, even when it breaks your egocentric little scribbler's heart, kill your darlings)". [verified: secondary, signalvnoise] Note: "murder your darlings" originates with Arthur Quiller-Couch (1914); King is quoting the tradition.
- First draft of a book should take no more than about three months; longer and the story "begins to take on an odd foreign feel". [verified: secondary, signalvnoise]
- Paul Hirsch to King: "A movie should be there in rough cut"; King: "The same is true of books." and "I think it's rare that incoherence or dull storytelling can be solved by something so minor as a second draft." [verified: secondary, signalvnoise]
- "The situation comes first. The characters - always flat and unfeatured, to begin with - come next." [verified: secondary, willpatrick.co.uk]
- "Stories are found things, like fossils in the ground" [verified: secondary, multiple quote collections (wist.info, kwize); exact page not checked]
- "I believe the road to hell is paved with adverbs, and I will shout it from the rooftops." [verified: secondary, multiple sources including The Marginalian 2013 and vocabulary.com]
- Description: "Thin description leaves the reader feeling bewildered and nearsighted. Overdescription buries him or her in details and images." [verified: secondary, willpatrick.co.uk]
- Ideal Reader (IR): write for one specific person whose taste you know. [paraphrase, verified: secondary]
- "The scariest moment is always just before you start." [verified: secondary, signalvnoise]

## Charles Bukowski
Source for most lines: On Writing (Ecco, 2015, ed. Abel Debritto), letters 1945 to 1993, quoted with recipient and date by calvinrosser.com. [verified: secondary; not checked against the printed book]
- To Jon Webb, late Jan 1961: "It's when you begin to lie to yourself in a poem in order to simply make a poem, that you fail."
- To Jon Webb, late Jan 1961: "The only thing intelligent about a good art is if it shakes you alive, otherwise it's hokum."
- To Harold Norse, 12 May 1964: "It's best to stay loose, work wild and easy and fail any way you want to."
- To William Packard, 13 Oct 1972: "Hemingway lived hard but got locked in his craft too and after a while his craft became a cage and killed him."
- To Robert Head and Darlene Fife, 23 May 1973: "The first process of learning or creation is the undoing of teaching."
- To Mike Golden, 4 Nov 1980: "Because you're not accepted doesn't necessarily mean you're a genius. Maybe you just write badly."
- To William Packard, 19 May 1984: "Endurance is more important than truth because without endurance there can't be any truth."
- To Carl Weissner, 6 Nov 1988: "When you write only to get famous you shit it away."
- To Colorado North Review, 15 Sep 1990: "A writer is only a writer if he can write now, tonight, this minute."
- Letter to William Packard, about 1990, published by Letters of Note: "Don't try." [verified: secondary, Flavorwire 2010 quoting the letter; date approximate]
- Poem "so you want to be a writer?": widely reproduced; I did not check a print edition, so the kit paraphrases it instead of quoting.

## R. L. Stine
- NPR interview (transcript 449748255, 2015): "I always start with a title." Titles lead to the story. "every chapter is a cliffhanger". "Whenever I have something that I think is kind of intense, I throw in something funny to lighten up." "I don't really want to terrify kids." [verified: primary transcript]
- Parade interview, 23 May 2025 (republished on AOL): "I have to start with a title. Think of the title, and then it leads me to a story." "I outline every book. Everyone hates outlines... but I can't work without it." "I spend about a week on the outline." Outline format: "Chapter One. This is what happens. This is the chapter ending." "So when I sit down to write the book, I know everything that's going to happen." Writing takes about three weeks. [verified: primary interview text]

## Wilbur Smith
- Strand Magazine interview, 15 May 2018: "I start a novel when I am sure about how to write the opening, after months and months of research where I live in the world I want to write about." "I never take notes. If it's worth remembering, you don't need to write it down." "Then I set off, letting my imagination roam around the story archipelago in my mind." Characters: chosen "from real life and then embroidering them a bit more". [verified: primary]
- Gulf News (page dated 2022): "And I write for myself, not others. I have to please myself." Sagas about families because "I know the characters already." Works "businessman's hours - five days a week, 8am to three or four in the afternoon". "good adventure writing is picking a person or characters that are from life". [verified: primary interview text]
- BooksLive 2018 (via writerswrite.co.za): "I know how the story will end, but my characters guide me there." [verified: secondary]

## Steven Spielberg
- Raiders of the Lost Ark story conference transcript, January 1978 (Lucas, Spielberg, Kasdan), PDF hosted by American University: [verified: primary]
  - Lucas: "He's a real professional. He's really good. And that is the key to the whole thing."
  - Lucas: "If we don't make him vulnerable, he's got no problems." Kasdan: "Some sorrow in our guy, who is very cool and you never see it."
  - Spielberg: "As long as he has brains. He should be able to talk his way out of things."
  - Lucas: "The trouble with cliff hangers is, you get somebody into something, you sort have to get them out in a plausible way." Spielberg: "And each cliffhanger is better than the one before." Lucas: "That is the progression we have to do."
  - Lucas: "We want to keep things interspaced and at the same time build it."
  - Spielberg on Marion: "She's not just somebody to be around for comic relief or romantic relief." Lucas: "if she's forced into it, you're constantly fighting to try and keep her there."
  - Lucas on Belloq: rivals who "respect each other as archeologists and as opponents."
- Jaws: on BBC Radio 4 Desert Island Discs (reported by thedailyjaws.com, 2022): the shark breaking down meant "figuring out how to create suspense and terror without seeing the shark itself"; Hitchcock "was able to scare you without really seeing anything"; "it is a scarier movie without seeing so much of the shark." [verified: secondary report of a broadcast]
- Schindler's List red coat, from The Hollywood Reporter oral history (via SlashFilm): "It was as obvious as a little girl in red." He meant the Allies' leaders had information about the camps and did nothing. [verified: secondary]
- Saving Private Ryan, American Cinematographer Aug 1998, Kaminski: shot to look like "it was shot on 16mm by a bunch of combat cameramen"; handheld for "about 90 percent"; shutter at 45 or 90 degrees; desaturated; "ugly lighting and ugly composition tells the story much better than the perfect light." Spielberg often covered a scene in one continuous shot. [verified: primary trade article]
- Film observations used in the kit (watchable primary sources, described not quoted): Duel never shows the truck driver's face; Jaws keeps the shark mostly unseen until late and gives Brody a fear of water that the climax forces him into; Munich builds each assassination as a set piece with an innocent complication; Lincoln runs on a vote count as a clock; Bridge of Spies repeats Abel's question "Would it help?" as a motif; Titanic is Cameron, see below.

## Stanley Kubrick
- Michel Ciment interview on Barry Lyndon (Kubrick: The Definitive Edition; transcript on The Kubrick Site, copy read): "Good research is an absolute necessity and I enjoy doing it." "I spent a year preparing Barry Lyndon". "The starting point and sine qua non of any historical or futuristic story is to make you believe what you see." Narration: "There is too much story to tell"; voice-over is "a perfectly legitimate and economical way of conveying story information". "Barry Lyndon is a story which does not depend upon surprise." "What is important is not what is going to happen, but how it will happen." "Being told in advance of the impending disaster gives away surprise but creates suspense." The symmetry "arose primarily out of the needs of telling the story rather than as part of a conscious design." Filmmaking "is as much a matter of discovery as it is the execution of a plan." [verified: primary transcript copy]
- "Non-submersible units": reported by Brian Aldiss (A Life in Pictures; Stanley and Us): Kubrick said a film needs about six "non-submersible units" and to forget the connections for the moment. [verified: secondary report of a private remark]
- McKee (Story, ch. 6) credits Kubrick's antiwar films with researching the counter-idea (human beings love to fight) instead of preaching.

## Ridley Scott
- American Cinematographer, July 1982 (Blade Runner): Scott: things get "retrofit" on the outside of buildings because rebuilding costs too much; "You wear your guts on the outside." Syd Mead: an "overlaying process of putting pipes and ductwork and signs on the buildings"; "a Hong Kong or Calcutta kind of density that Ridley was after"; "a messy patina"; "What would an automatic fork cleaner look like in 40 years?"; "People will believe something is real only if it is done right and compared to what they think it should be." Scott: "It's not a film about genetics. It touches on it, but it isn't about it." [verified: primary trade article]
- Alien's lived-in crew (talk of money and contracts, clutter): analysed by McKee (Story ch. 8) as Dan O'Bannon's script work; the "truckers" reading is McKee's.

## Danny Boyle
- DGA Quarterly, Summer 2015: "I really like to do something different each time." "I always try and get a lot of rehearsal". Style: "You really find it as you're shooting." Quotes "Thou shalt not bore" as Billy Wilder's. Mentor Alan Clarke: "Watch the rushes." On Steve Jobs: "we decided to make a film where if anyone sat down it had enormous significance." [verified: primary]
- Yorkshire Post (about 2013): "I like films to be visceral - to burst in the door at the beginning". "you try and find the absolute style of the film from the story, if you can." "I set the story up as a voyage that we're all going on." [verified: primary interview text]

## James Cameron
- Scriptments: hybrid of treatment and script; Cameron wrote them for Spider-Man (57 pages), Titanic (131 pages), Avatar (1994, about 80 pages). Mostly summarized present-tense action, slug lines only for major sequences, short dialogue embedded, longer exchanges formatted. [verified: secondary, Wikipedia "Scriptment" with cited sources]
- Titanic screenplay: early scene in the imaging shack where Bodine shows Rose a computer animation of the sinking ("She hits the berg on the starboard side and it sort of bumps along..."). The audience learns the mechanics of the disaster before living it. [verified: primary, screenplay text]
- Avatar sequels: "I plopped down 800 pages of notes, single spaced." The bar: "It had to hit the heart, had to hit the mind, had to hit the imagination". (ScreenRant via Go Into The Story) [verified: secondary]
- Terminator (Cameron and Gale Anne Hurd): McKee (ch. 16, 17) analyses the closing tape-recorder scene that defuses the time paradox, and the Terminator's eye-repair and insult menu as love for a villain.

## Ron Howard
- DGA Quarterly, Fall 2009: "every story should be a suspense story". "all stories are really about coping". "for me, making movies is so much about character." On Apollo 13: "To put people on the edge of their seats they have to fully understand what's going on." "I don't direct just to get a result". "I encouraged a lot of spontaneity with the secondary characters". [verified: primary]

## Robert Zemeckis and Bob Gale
- Zemeckis, DGA Quarterly, Spring 2013, on Back to the Future: "Everything is set up, everything is paid off." Every line, beat, cut and shot "is doing what movies are supposed to do." On Forrest Gump's script: "there was no traditional dramatic convention propelling the story; just the characters." "I work really hard to try to keep film technique invisible." [verified: primary]
- Gale, SYFY Wire, June 2026 (annotated screenplay): "you don't get it right the first time, the second time, the third time, or even the fourth time." Jokes grounded in "Something Marty McFly would have seen in 1985". [verified: primary interview text]

## Charlie Kaufman, BAFTA Screenwriters' Lecture, 30 Sep 2011 (BAFTA transcript)
- "Say who you are, really say it in your life and in your work."
- "Failure is a badge of honour; it means you risked failure."
- "There's no template for a screenplay, or there shouldn't be." We've been "conned into thinking there is a pre-established form."
- "I think the danger of craft is that it needs to be in second position to what it is that you're doing."
- "Movies share so much with dreams". "It's very important that what you do is specific to the medium in which you're doing it."
- "Allow yourself the freedom to change as you discover". "Do not simplify." [verified: primary transcript]

## Alex Garland
- The Ringer, 23 Feb 2018: meaning is "done by inference, not by statement. It requires participation by the audience". "I often try to reach a point in scripts where they become dialogue-less." Exposition is often "unmotivated by any truth of the character". "Just write the thing that grabs you, the thing you're obsessed with". On Annihilation: "I don't know how to do a faithful adaptation of your book." [verified: primary interview text]
- Creative Screenwriting, Jan 2016: on Ex Machina, "I was trying to not show my hand". "ultimately your loyalty is to the film, not the novel." [verified: primary interview text]

## Ed Catmull and the Pixar Braintrust
- Stanford eCorner "Inside the Braintrust" transcript: "The brain trust had no authority." "They could not tell the director what to do." "We need a lot of candor about what works and what doesn't work." People "don't want to embarrass themselves". The director wasn't entering "in a defensive posture". [verified: primary transcript]
- First Round Review interview: "You don't have to listen to anyone's notes, not even ours." "Just because we say, 'Story is king,' doesn't make it true." [verified: primary interview text]
- Masters of Scale: "this really needs to be composed just to filmmakers"; "you just have to be truthful"; "We had to remove the power from the room"; "They couldn't override the director." "There isn't the recipe". [verified: primary]
- Salon 2014: "all of our films start out as disasters". Notes are only a reminder "because it never catches what's in the room." [verified: primary]
- McKinsey Quarterly, 29 Mar 2016: Disney in the 1990s thought it had "found a template to consistently produce good movies": "every film was a musical with five to seven songs and a funny sidekick". "Spectacular success doesn't lead to deep introspection, which in turn leads to wrong conclusions." First attempts reach only a B; you get to A by responding to problems revealed in each attempt. "We have to try extra hard to make it safe to fail." [verified: primary]
- NOT FOUND: a statement by Catmull defending classical Hollywood structure or warning against breaking it. The verified record points the other way: he warns against templates. The kit reports this instead of inventing the position.

## Added sources (each fills one gap)
- Aristotle, Poetics: the origin of reversal, recognition, unity of action, and "probable impossibility" over "improbable possibility". Used through McKee's citations plus the text itself. Justification: McKee builds on it; it is the oldest test of whether structure is universal.
- William Goldman, Adventures in the Screen Trade (1983): "Nobody knows anything." [verified: secondary, Den of Geek]. "Screenplays are structure" [unverified exact wording: widely attributed, not confirmed]. Goldman's ending principle, give the audience what it wants but not the way it expects, is [verified: primary, cited in McKee ch. 13]. Justification: the only working screenwriter's view of structure in the source list.
- Chakrabarty et al., "Can AI writing be salvaged? Mitigating Idiosyncrasies and Improving Human-AI Alignment in the Writing Process through Edits" (arXiv 2409.14509, CHI 2025). 18 writers edited 1,057 LLM paragraphs (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1 70B), 8,035 edits. Seven idiosyncrasies: Cliché; Unnecessary/Redundant Exposition; Purple Prose; Poor Sentence Structure; Lack of Specificity and Detail; Awkward Word Choice and Phrasing; Tense Inconsistency. No significant quality difference between the three models. Recurring phrases: "sense of", "mix of", "weight of". [verified: primary paper]. Justification: the only controlled study of AI fiction tells found; it replaces listicles.
- Wikipedia "Signs of AI writing" (WikiProject AI Cleanup): attempted, fetch failed. Not used as a source.
