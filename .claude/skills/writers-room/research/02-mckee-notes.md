# McKee, Story (1997): chapter notes

Read from the supplied epub (It Books edition). Paraphrased notes. Short quotes are marked with quotation marks and are exact from the text. Line numbers refer to the plain-text conversion and are only for internal checking.

## Introduction
- Principles, not rules. A rule dictates; a principle describes what has always worked. Anxious writers obey rules, rebels break them, artists master form.
- Universal forms, not formulas. No paradigm or foolproof model exists.
- Archetype vs stereotype: archetypal story finds a universal human experience and dresses it in culture-specific, never-before-seen detail. Stereotype does the opposite: narrow culture-bound experience dressed in generic detail. "Stereotypical stories stay at home, archetypal stories travel." Audience pleasure is double: discovering an unknown world, then finding themselves in it.
- Thoroughness: a screenplay can take as long as a novel; economy takes time.
- The camera exposes anything false. The screenwriter cannot hide behind explanatory language.
- Write for quality, not the market; secure writers rewrite until the script is director-ready.
- Talented people write badly when blinded by an idea to prove or an emotion to vent; they write well when driven to move the audience. The audience is smarter than most films; the writer must stay ahead of it.
- Originality = distinctive content plus distinctive shaping. Content and form shape each other. Eccentricity is not originality; difference for its own sake is as empty as following commercial rules. A mature artist does not call attention to himself or break convention merely because it is convention.
- Story structure reveals the writer's personal cosmology: his view of how and why things happen.

## Ch 1 The Story Problem
- Stories as "equipment for living" (Kenneth Burke, quoted by McKee). Audiences seek patterns of meaning experienced emotionally.
- Spectacle substitutes for substance when story fails.
- Story analyst's standard rejection: fat expository first 30 pages, coincidence, weak motivation, no protagonist, subplots that never form, characters never more than they seem, no insight. The sign on the door says Story Department. Bad story causes bland text; good story tends to produce sharper dialogue and images.
- 75 percent or more of the labor is designing story. Core questions: who are they, what do they want, why, how do they try, what stops them, what are the consequences.
- Story form is universal; formula is not.
- Craft frees the subconscious: write, then step out and analyze; alternate.
- Two failed scripts: the understructured "personal story" (slice of life, verisimilitude mistaken for truth) and the overstructured "commercial" spectacle (kinesis mistaken for entertainment). Facts are neutral: "But it actually happened" is the weakest reason to include anything. "What happens is fact, not truth. Truth is what we think about what happens."
- Story is metaphor for life: abstract from life to find essence, without becoming an abstraction.
- Literary talent (common) vs story talent (rare). Audiences prefer trivial material told brilliantly over profound material told badly. Story talent primary, literary talent secondary but essential.

## Ch 2 The Structure Spectrum
- The mark of a master: select a few moments that give us a lifetime (Tender Mercies starts at rock bottom; the past arrives in and between scenes).
- Structure: a selection of events from the characters' life stories composed into a strategic sequence to arouse specific emotions and express a specific view of life.
- Story Event: meaningful change in a character's life situation, expressed in terms of a value, achieved through conflict (coincidental rain does not count; the rainmaker's struggle does).
- Story values: binary qualities of experience that can flip charge (alive/dead, love/hate, truth/lie, hope/despair...).
- Scene: action through conflict in roughly continuous time and space that turns at least one value. Typical film: 40 to 60 scenes. Test each scene: value at the top, value at the end. Same charge = nonevent, usually exposition; cut it and weave the information elsewhere. Ideal: no scene that doesn't turn.
- A scene can travel through several locations (bedroom, kitchen, garage, highway) and still be one scene if it could be played "in one".
- Beat: exchange of behavior in action/reaction; beats shape the turn. Example scene has six beats (teasing, insults, threat/dare, plea/ridicule, violence, decision).
- Sequence: 2 to 5 scenes culminating in greater impact than any previous scene. Title each sequence by its purpose (NO JOB to JOB).
- Act: series of sequences peaking in a major reversal. Story: series of acts building to a last-act climax of absolute, irreversible change. Earlier changes can be reversed; the story climax cannot.
- Every phrase of dialogue or line of description either turns behavior and action or sets up the conditions for change.
- Plot: the writer's choice of events and their design in time. All stories are plotted, including inner ones (Tender Mercies).
- Movies make "mental things physical" (John Carpenter, quoted by McKee).
- Story triangle:
  - Archplot (Classical Design): active protagonist, primarily external antagonism, pursuing desire, continuous time, consistent causal reality, closed ending of absolute irreversible change.
  - Miniplot: reduces the classical elements: open ending possible, internal conflict, multiple protagonists possible, passive protagonist. Still must satisfy as story.
  - Antiplot: reverses the classical: nonlinear time, coincidence over causality, inconsistent realities. Tends to extravagance.
- Seven formal contrasts: closed vs open ending; external vs internal conflict; single vs multiple protagonists; active vs passive protagonist; linear vs nonlinear time; causality vs coincidence; consistent vs inconsistent reality. These are spectrums; films slide along them (Barton Fink sits in the middle).
- Open ending: one or two questions left, some emotion left to the audience, but answerable within clear limited alternatives.
- Passive protagonist is compensated by strong inner struggle or by dramatic events around him.
- Nonplot: below the line, no change, portraiture. Outside story.
- Politics: Hollywood tends toward forced up-endings, art film toward fashionable darkness; truth sits between.
- Audience shrinks as design moves toward Miniplot/Antiplot. Not about quality: most people experience life as Archplot (single active protagonist, external conflict, causal, closed). "Classical design is a mirror of the human mind." It models memory and anticipation. It is not Western; it is human.
- Budget must shrink with audience (Robbe-Grillet kept films under $750,000).
- Miniplot and Antiplot are reactions to the Classical; to bend it you must understand it. Bergman, Fellini, Godard, Altman mastered classical form first.
- Write only what you believe. Breaking Hollywood rules to look like an artist is delinquency, not creativity.

## Ch 3 Structure and Setting
- The source of all clichés: the writer does not know the world of his story.
- Setting has four dimensions: Period, Duration, Location, Level of Conflict (inner, personal, institutional, environmental). The cast is part of the setting.
- Setting confines possibility: a story must obey its own laws of probability. Fantasy gets one leap then strict rules. Once the audience learns the rules, breaking them feels like a violation.
- "There is no such thing as a portable story." An honest story belongs to one place and time.
- Principle of creative limitation: small, knowable world. Know it so well no question about it goes unanswered. Small is not trivial.
- Larger world = thinner knowledge = more cliché.
- Research of memory (relive and write it down), imagination (live the character's day), fact (library). Writer's block = nothing to say; feed talent with research. Research confirms experience is universal and expands it.
- Research feeds events; it is not a substitute for story and must not become procrastination. Invention and investigation alternate.
- Creativity is five, ten, twenty to one. Cure cliché by listing 5 to 15 versions of a scene, then choosing the truest to character and world that has not been seen. If the cliché setting is right, list variations inside it and research until you know it better than anyone.
- Taste and will to throw away banalities, conceits, false notes and lies.

## Ch 4 Structure and Genre
- Aristotle's four genres by ending charge x design (Simple/Complex, Fortunate/Tragic). Friedman added Education, Redemption, Disillusionment plots.
- The audience is already a genre expert. Two-handed trick: fulfill anticipations and lead them to unexpected moments. Requires genre knowledge beyond the audience's.
- Working genre list (25): Love Story (Buddy Salvation); Horror (Uncanny, Supernatural, Super-Uncanny); Modern Epic (individual vs state); Western; War; Maturation; Redemption (bad to good); Punitive (good turns bad, punished); Testing (willpower vs temptation); Education (negative to positive worldview); Disillusionment (positive to negative worldview); Comedy (many subgenres); Crime (subgenre set by whose POV); Social Drama (problem plus cure; Domestic, Woman's, Political, Eco, Medical, Psycho); Action/Adventure (High Adventure, Disaster/Survival). Supra-genres: Historical Drama, Biography (incl. Autobiography), Docudrama, Mockumentary, Musical, Science Fiction, Sports, Fantasy, Animation, Art Film (Minimalism, Antistructure).
- Historical Drama: "What is past must be present." The only justification for the past is to show the present through it.
- Autobiography often lacks the self-knowledge it promises.
- "We're all genre writers." Identify genre, research its conventions.
- Genre conventions: specific settings, roles, events, values. Some genres pliable (Disillusionment), some rigid (Crime: crime early, detective, criminal "makes it personal", apprehension).
- Comedy's overriding convention: nobody really gets hurt (the Wanda terrier example: gore killed the laugh). Black Comedy allows sharp but bearable pain.
- Art Film's grand convention is unconventionality.
- Genre study method: list similar works (successes and failures), watch with script in hand, break each into setting, role, event, value, stack the analyses, ask what the genre always does.
- Mispositioned audience kills a film (Mike's Murder).
- Creative limitation: conventions are the rhyme scheme. Keep convention, avoid cliché (boy meets girl is convention; how they meet is where cliché lives).
- Action convention: hero at mercy of villain must turn the tables. Fresh solutions: Raiders (shrug, gun), Die Hard (gun taped to back).
- Mixing genres creates new forms. Genres evolve with society (Western, Psycho-Drama in four stages, Love Story's force opposing love). Antique values bore (Falling in Love).
- Chinatown broke the convention that the detective punishes the criminal; possible only because society had changed. A classic is reinterpretable by each generation.
- Endurance: a screenplay takes 6 months to a year or more. Great writers focus on one subject through a lifetime. Write in the genre you love; love of the work sustains you.

## Ch 5 Structure and Character
- Structure vs character is a false debate: structure is character; character is structure.
- Characterization: all observable traits. True character: revealed in choices under pressure; the greater the pressure, the truer the revelation. Choices without risk mean nothing.
- Fundamental principle: true character revealed in contrast or contradiction to characterization. Principals must not be at heart what they seem on the surface (Rambo flattened in sequels; Bond endures because the mask and the core differ).
- Finest writing also arcs true character over the telling (The Verdict). Hamlet pattern: characterization, then true nature revealed through choice, nature contrasts with surface, increasing pressure forces harder choices, climax has changed him.
- Function of structure: progressively building pressure forcing harder risk-taking choices, revealing true nature down to the unconscious. Function of characterization: credibility for those choices.
- Change the event, you change the character; change deep character, you must reinvent the events. "Character-driven" is redundant; often means characterization-driven portraiture.
- Complexity of character must fit genre (simple for action/farce, complex for education/redemption).
- "Movies are about their last twenty minutes." Save the best for last; film is temporal art.
- 75 percent of effort goes into story design, and 75 percent of that into the last-act climax. Until you have the climax you don't have a story.
- If characterization undermines the climax's credibility, change characterization (Jake 75 becomes 35), keep deep character. Greed's handcuff ending.

## Ch 6 Structure and Meaning
- Aesthetic emotion: in life, idea and emotion arrive separately; story fuses them at the moment of the event. Story expresses ideas through senses and emotion, not argument.
- Premise: the open question or image that starts the writing ("What would happen if..."; Bergman's Persona from vertigo and two faces). A premise is not precious; abandon it if the story turns. Writing is discovery.
- Storytelling is "the creative demonstration of truth." The event structure proves the idea "without explanation." Master storytellers dramatize, never explain. Dialogue is not a platform for the author's philosophy.
- The meaning must be dramatized in the climax without explanatory dialogue ("They got the bastard!" instead of "crime doesn't pay").
- Controlling Idea: one sentence, Value plus Cause, describing how and why life changes from opening to end. "Poverty", "war", "love" are not themes. The clearer the single idea, the more meanings the audience finds; too many ideas implode.
- Examples: Dirty Harry (justice through greater violence) vs Columbo (justice through cleverness): the controlling idea decides which scenes are appropriate.
- Find it from the climax: what value does the final action bring, and what is the chief cause. The story tells you its meaning; you don't dictate it. Self-recognition: the climax may reveal things about you that surprise you. If the plot works out exactly as planned, you are not working loosely enough.
- Idea vs Counter-Idea: progressions swing between positive and negative charge of the values, sequence by sequence. At crisis they collide; the climax decides which wins (Chayefsky's note taped to the typewriter).
- Didacticism: build both sides with equal force. A weak antagonist makes the win meaningless. Writing to prove an idea risks the thesis film. Antiwar films rarely convince because they ignore that men love war; Kubrick's antiwar films work because they research the counter-idea in human nature.
- Idealist (up), Pessimist (down), Ironist (up/down) controlling ideas. Positive irony: compulsive pursuit of a value will destroy you, but seeing it in time and throwing it away redeems you (protagonist wins by "losing"; how to make a non-action feel positive). Negative irony: cling to the obsession, you get it, it destroys you.
- Irony lasts longest and travels widest because life is ironic. Irony is not ambiguity (a blur) and not coincidence; it is a clear double statement of what is gained and lost, kept separate in the audience's experience.
- No story is innocent; all coherent tales carry an idea inside an emotional spell (Plato, Death Wish). The writer's only responsibility is to tell the truth: check the controlling idea and ask if you believe it.

## Ch 7 The Substance of Story
- Language is a medium, not the substance. The substance is felt, not seen.
- Protagonist can be single, plural (shared desire, shared consequences), or multi (separate desires; becomes Multiplot). Non-human possible. Protagonist switch is possible but rare (Psycho).
- Hallmarks of the protagonist: willful; has a conscious desire (he could tell you what he wants); may have a self-contradictory unconscious desire (the most memorable ones do; Blanche DuBois says she wants reality, unconsciously wants escape); has the capacities to pursue the desire convincingly; must have at least a chance; has the will and capacity to go to the end of the line set by setting and genre.
- "A STORY must build to a final action beyond which the audience cannot imagine another." Audiences shouldn't leave rewriting the ending.
- "The PROTAGONIST must be empathetic; he may or may not be sympathetic." Sympathy = likable. Empathy = "like me": the audience recognizes shared humanity and wants him to get what he wants for its own reasons. Empathy is absolute, sympathy optional. Likability is just characterization; audiences bond with deep character revealed through choice. Macbeth made empathetic by conscience. Interview with the Vampire's Louis loses the audience because he whines and could end it: audiences detach from hypocrites.
- The first step: every character always takes the minimum, conservative action from his point of view to get what he wants (minimum is relative to who he is). In life the world usually cooperates. In story, never: the action provokes forces of antagonism; the world reacts differently than expected, more powerfully, or both.
- Three levels of conflict: inner (mind, body, emotion), personal (intimates beyond social roles), extra-personal (institutions, individuals in social roles, environment, time, space).
- The Gap: story is born where subjective expectation and objective result split. Probability (what we expect) vs necessity (what actually happens when we act). After the gap, the character must take a second, harder action that puts him at risk: he must risk losing something to gain something.
- Risk test: what is the worst that happens if the protagonist fails? If "life goes back to normal", the story is not worth telling. Value of a desire is proportional to the risk taken to achieve it.
- Progression: each action opens a bigger gap, demanding more will, more capacity, more risk, until a final action beyond which none can be imagined.
- Action vs activity: action opens gaps and causes significant change; activity is behavior where the expected happens.
- Writing from the inside out: emotional truth comes only from yourself. Ask "If I were this character in these circumstances, what would I do?" (Stanislavski's Magic If). Not "how should someone" (moralizing), "how might someone" (cute), "what would he do" (guessing, cliché), or "what would I do" (your truth, not his).
- Chinatown Act Two climax (Towne) walked beat by beat, each character's POV, each beat an expectation broken. Critical rewrite note: the third draft had Evelyn explain the incest backstory at length; it was cut to Gittes's "He raped you?" and her silent "no", which keeps Cross's cruelty intact and tests Gittes. Exposition that softens the antagonist weakens the story.
- Method: create an honest moment from one POV, step out, ask "What is the opposite of that?", invade another POV to create the unexpected reaction; repeat.
- "Fine writing emphasizes REACTIONS." Genre makes many actions expected; quality is in to whom, why and how it happens. A beat where expectation equals result (knock, door opens politely) is a pace killer the editor will cut.
- The substance and the energy of story are the gap. When the gap opens for the character, it opens for the audience (the "Oh my God" moment).

## Ch 8 The Inciting Incident
- Five-part design: Inciting Incident, Progressive Complications, Crisis, Climax, Resolution.
- World questions: how they make a living (we only show work when it causes more problems than it solves); politics (distribution of power, including inside families and love: "He who confesses first loses"); rituals (meals etc.); values (good/evil, right/wrong, legal/illegal are not the same thing); genre conventions; biographies; Backstory (significant past events the writer can use to build progressions, not the life history); cast design.
- Cast design principle: polarization. If something happens at dinner, every character reacts differently. Two characters with the same attitude: merge them or cut one (Jeff's Iowa dinner: family cheering vs Dad, Mom, Sister each with a different agenda).
- Author, authority, authenticity. Empathy and authenticity control audience involvement. Authenticity is internal consistency, not actuality. Aristotle (as quoted by McKee): a convincing impossibility is preferable to an unconvincing possibility.
- Alien's crew are truck drivers (money, contract, collectibles); acid blood from researching the creature's biology. Believability is the key to emotion.
- The "telling detail": a few selected details let the audience complete the picture; trying too hard to be real breaks belief.
- Originality lies in the struggle for authenticity, not eccentricity. Style can't be achieved self-consciously.
- Inciting Incident must be dynamic and fully developed (not "I'm bored, I'll move"). It "radically upsets the balance of forces in the protagonist's life", positively or negatively. Usually happens directly to or is caused by the protagonist. May need setup and payoff, but the payoff cannot be delayed (The River's failure).
- Protagonist must react (even refusal is a reaction, but it can't last). The incident arouses a desire to restore balance; he conceives an Object of Desire; he pursues it. Complex protagonists also get an unconscious desire contradicting the conscious one (Carnal Knowledge, Mrs. Soffel, The Crying Game).
- Spine (through-line, super-objective): the protagonist's deep desire and effort to restore balance; unifies every scene. If there is an unconscious desire, it is the spine, and the conscious desire may change.
- The Quest: an event throws life out of balance, arousing desire for what will restore it, launching a quest against forces of antagonism (inner, personal, extra-personal). He may or may not achieve it. That is story in a nutshell.
- Inciting Incident happens by coincidence or by decision (protagonist's or someone with power over him). Central plot's Inciting Incident must happen onscreen: it raises the Major Dramatic Question and projects the Obligatory Scene (the Crisis: confrontation with the most powerful forces of antagonism). Having promised it, you must show it (Jaws: sheriff and shark face to face, not reported).
- Interior genres reveal the Obligatory Scene slowly (Tender Mercies: daughter's death tests the change; declarations of change convince no one).
- Placement: within the first 25 percent; "as soon as possible... but not until the moment is ripe": when the audience knows enough to react fully. Archetypal incidents need no setup (Kramer, Jaws, Metamorphosis). Rocky delays to make "Him? That loser?!" possible. Late central plot requires a setup subplot.
- Common fault: delaying the central plot with exposition; underestimating the audience. Bergman's economy (Through a Glass Darkly).
- Quality: can be as small as French toast scraped into the disposal (Ordinary People) if it performs the functions. Checklist: upsets balance; arouses desire to restore; conscious object; unconscious contradicting desire for complex protagonists; launches quest; raises Major Dramatic Question; projects Obligatory Scene.
- Creating it: worst thing that could happen, how it becomes the best (Kramer); best thing, how it becomes the worst (Death in Venice, Godfather II). Can cycle more than once. Second hardest scene after the climax; rewritten most.

## Ch 9 Act Design
- Progressive Complications: more conflict against greater forces, passing points of no return. After each gap, actions of that magnitude are eliminated. "A story must not retreat to actions of lesser quality or magnitude." Soft middle of Act Two = recycling actions of Act One size.
- Feature Archplot: 40 to 60 scenes, 12 to 18 sequences, 3 or more acts. Invent hundreds of scenes to choose 40 to 60.
- Law of Conflict: "Nothing moves forward in a story except through conflict." Conflict is to story what sound is to music. Quantity of conflict in life is constant; remove it from one level and it bulges on another (boredom is inner conflict). Failure modes: glut of meaningless violent conflict, or vacancy of honest conflict.
- Complication (conflict on one level: action = extra-personal, soap = personal, stream of consciousness = inner; needs big casts and many locations) vs Complexity (conflict on all three levels at once). Kramer's French toast scene: three values (self-confidence, son's trust, domestic survival) all turn positive to negative in three minutes with little dialogue.
- Advice: relatively simple but complex stories. Don't proliferate characters; don't multiply locations.
- Act lengths: 1 act for 5 to 20 minute works; 2 acts for sitcom/novella/hour plays; feature needs at least 3 major reversals ("things were good, then bad: end" feels incomplete). Act One about 25 percent (climax at 20 to 30 min of 120); last act shortest, 20 minutes or less, accelerating. Example: Inciting Incident at minute 1, Act One climax at 30, 70-minute Act Two, 18-minute Act Three, 2-minute resolution.
- Long Act Two cures: subplots (with their own act structures, reversals falling inside Act Two), or more acts: Mid-Act Climax (four acts), five acts (Four Weddings), seven (Raiders), eight. Costs: more climaxes invite cliché; repeated life-and-death climaxes stop feeling major ("That's his day").
- Three-act central plot plus subplots is the common standard because it fits most writers' powers.
- Design variations: number of reversals; placement of inciting incident (late incident becomes Act One climax; must be justified by audience need, not writer convenience).
- False Ending: seems complete, isn't (Terminator double). Hitchcock used them early. Usually inappropriate; better: penultimate climax intensifies the question.
- Act rhythm: last two act climaxes must not repeat charge. Up ending needs a negative penultimate climax and vice versa. Irony must lean one way; penultimate contradicts the lean.
- Subplots: contradict the controlling idea (irony), resonate it (variation), set up a late central plot, or complicate the central plot (most important; e.g. love stories inside crime stories). A subplot that does none of these splits the film (The First Deadly Sin adaptation: wife in hospital never connects to killer).
- Multiplot: no central spine; held together by an idea (Parenthood: you cannot win at parenting; Diner: men can't talk to women).
- Screenwriting makes the mental physical: visual correlatives for inner conflict, not narration.
- Kiss of the Spider Woman reveals its backstory inciting incident at mid-act because opening with Luis agreeing to spy would kill empathy. "You're free to break or bend convention, but for one reason only: to put something more important in its place."

## Ch 10 Scene Design
- Scene = story in miniature: desire, action, conflict, change. Scene-Objective must be an aspect of the Spine. Ideal: every scene a minor, moderate or major Turning Point; never a flat expository scene.
- Turning Point effects: surprise, increased curiosity ("Why?"), insight (audience races back through the story and finds answers layered in), new direction. Examples: Trading Places, Wall Street ("Tell me something I don't know"), Kramer's breakfast (he's a child), Empire ("I'm your father"), Chinatown.
- Self-expression lives in how you turn the story, not in the words. Weak storytelling substitutes information for insight and explains meaning through dialogue or voice-over, giving characters "phony, self-conscious knowledge".
- Setups/Payoffs: plant knowledge that has one meaning on first sight and a second (third, fourth) meaning after the turn (Cross's "My daughter"). Too subtle: missed. Too heavy: seen coming. "Turning Points fail when we overprepare the obvious and underprepare the unusual." Adjust firmness to audience. Every payoff becomes a new setup.
- "In storytelling, logic is retroactive." Think the unthinkable (brother betrays brother), then go back and layer it in. Imagination first, reasoning after.
- Emotion: there are two, pleasure and pain. Audience emotion requires empathy, knowing the want, understanding the values at stake; then a transition of values moves us. Emotion peaks and dissipates; the story must then turn in a new direction.
- Law of Diminishing Returns: repeated emotion back to back halves, then reverses (third tragic scene gets a laugh). Laughter is not an emotion; it is criticism of the ridiculous, needs setup and punch, and relief. Exception: positive to positive or negative to negative works if the contrast is huge (breakup then murder makes the breakup look positive).
- Feeling (mood) vs emotion: the scene's dynamic decides positive or negative; mood makes the emotion specific (same beats as comedy or thriller depending on light, casting, framing). Mood cannot substitute for emotion; exposition in pretty light is still boring.
- Nature of choice: a choice between good and evil, right and wrong, is no choice; people always choose the good as they see it. True choice is dilemma: irreconcilable goods, or lesser of two evils. Two-sided conflict (loves me, loves me not) vacillates and cannot end. Triangles create real choice, variety (20+ relationship variations) and closure: choosing B means sacrificing C. Strongest dilemmas combine both kinds (Dona Flor). Choice must be dilemma, not doubt.

## Ch 11 Scene Analysis
- When a scene fails, the problem is usually in the action (what characters do behind the mask), not the activity or the words. Rewriting dialogue won't fix it.
- Text = sensory surface. Subtext = thoughts and feelings, known and unknown, hidden by behavior. "Nothing is what it seems."
- Hollywood saying (quoted by McKee): "If the scene is about what the scene is about, you're in deep shit." On-the-nose = subtext written into the text. The candlelit "I love you" scene is unactable: nothing to act. Better: two people change a tire; romance lives underneath.
- Actors create from subtext; given none, good actors will invent one (candles come out when something is wrong).
- Everyone wears a mask; people who say exactly what they feel read as mad. Even confession hides a deeper layer (Evelyn's confession is really "Please help me"; Gittes's "We'll have to get you out of town" means "I believe you and I'll risk my life").
- Subtext applies to narration and direct address too: narrators lie and rationalize (Annie Hall). Alone, we still mask from ourselves.
- Five-step scene analysis: (1) define conflict: who drives the scene, what he wants as an infinitive; what the opposing force wants as an infinitive; they must be directly opposed. (2) opening value and charge. (3) break into beats: name each subtextual action with an emotive active gerund ("groveling at her feet", not "pleading"); a beat lasts until behavior changes. (4) closing value; compare; no change = nonevent. (5) survey beats for the arc and locate the Turning Point.
- Casablanca bazaar scene: 11 beats, each topping the last; turning point in beat 11 ("Victor Laszlo is my husband... even when I knew you in Paris"), a half-truth that implies her love was never real.
- Through a Glass Darkly: 20 beats, mostly physical; turning point between beats 13 and 14 (expected God, gets the spider). Synchronicity: coincidence acceptable when prepared by the character's emotional pitch.
- Failed scenes: desires not opposed (no conflict), repetitious/circular beats (antiprogressive), turning point too early or late (lopsided), on-the-nose dialogue (not credible).

## Ch 12 Composition
- Unity: "Because of the Inciting Incident, the Climax had to happen." The Spine binds them.
- Variety within unity: Casablanca is love story, political drama, action, comedy, near-musical. Seek the tragic in the comic, the political in the personal. Research cures monotony.
- Pacing: alternate tension and relaxation like the rhythm of a day. Lower tension after an act climax (comedy, romance), then top the previous climax. Constant rising tension exhausts the audience before the climax.
- Rhythm = scene length. 40 to 60 scenes in two hours, average 2.5 minutes, varied (one-minute scene, then a four-minute one). Average of five pages per scene drags. A camera exhausts a location in 2 to 3 minutes. French scenes: entrances and exits create new scenes inside one location (Twelve Angry Men has 80+).
- Tempo = activity level inside the scene. Toward act climaxes: shorter scenes, faster tempo; then let the climax itself breathe and slow ("earn the pause"). Pace begins in the screenplay; if you don't control it, the editor will.
- Expressing progression: Social progression (widen consequences into society; reason protagonists are often lawyers, doctors, politicians); Personal progression (drive down into intimate relationships and inner life, Ordinary People); both (Chinatown). Symbolic ascension (images go from particular to archetypal, never labeled: Deer Hunter's mountain, Terminator's labyrinth). Ironic ascension, six patterns: gets what he wanted too late; pushed away from the goal and led to it; throws away what he later needs; steps toward a goal that lead away; the act meant to destroy something destroys him; tries to get rid of what turns out to be happiness. Key: certainty and precision of the protagonist's plan.
- Transitions: link scene A to B by something held in common or in opposition (trait, action, object, word, light, sound, idea).

## Ch 13 Crisis, Climax, Resolution
- Crisis = the ultimate decision; the Obligatory Scene; facing the most powerful focused forces of antagonism; must be a true dilemma; reveals deepest character and the story's central value. Decisions take more willpower than actions.
- Climax built around one more turning point (Crisis within the Climax) is the most satisfying: he may or may not get it, but not as he expects (Empire's triple crisis vs Star Wars' straight action).
- Placement: Crisis and Climax usually together at the end. Climax can be extended action filling the last act (Casablanca's third act after Rick's decision). Crisis right after Inciting Incident (Bond, Leaving Las Vegas) risks repetition.
- If Crisis and Climax are in different places, cut directly from one to the other; cutting away drains energy.
- "The Crisis decision must be a deliberately static moment." Never offscreen; slow it down (Thelma & Louise stuttering over "go"; Deer Hunter's lowered rifle).
- "Meaning Produces Emotion." Climax: an absolute irreversible value swing, clear without explanation; dialogue that spells it out is redundant. Can be outwardly trivial (Ordinary People: she packs and leaves).
- Write backward from the climax: every scene must be justified by it; if it can be cut without hurting the ending, cut it.
- Climax subplots inside the main climax if possible (Rick's single action settles everything); otherwise least important first.
- Goldman (as cited by McKee): give the audience what it wants, but not the way it expects. What it wants is emotional satisfaction of the promised kind, not necessarily a happy ending; the writer sets the promise from the start. Aristotle (as cited): an ending must be "inevitable and unexpected".
- Truffaut (as cited): "Spectacle and Truth": a Key Image for the eye that concentrates the controlling idea (Greed, Sierra Madre, Seventh Seal, The Kid).
- Resolution: climax a leftover subplot (The In-Laws tweaks the main plot to keep tension), show the spread of effects across the cast, and always a "slow curtain" courtesy.

## Ch 14 The Principle of Antagonism
- "A protagonist and his story can only be as intellectually fascinating and emotionally compelling as the forces of antagonism make them." McKee calls this the most important and least understood principle; neglecting it is the main reason scripts fail.
- Forces of antagonism = sum of all opposing forces, not just a villain. At the Inciting Incident the protagonist should look like an underdog: a chance, only a chance; the total of all levels should seem overwhelming.
- Superman (Puzo): two rockets, one Superman; forced lesser-of-evils; then father's law vs Lois.
- Negative declension: Positive, Contrary (somewhat negative), Contradictory (direct opposite), Negation of the Negation (doubly negative, qualitatively worse). Justice: justice / unfairness / injustice / tyranny ("might makes right"). Love: love / indifference / hate / self-hate; or hate disguised as love (Ordinary People, Shine). Truth: truth / white lie / lie / self-deception. Consciousness: unconsciousness / death / damnation. Wealth: wealth / middle class / poverty / feeling poor while rich (Gekko). Communication: alienation / isolation / insanity. Achievement: compromise / failure / selling out. Intelligence: ignorance / stupidity / stupidity believed intelligent. Liberty: restraint / slavery / self-enslavement or slavery seen as freedom. Courage: fear / cowardice / cowardice that looks like courage. Loyalty: split allegiance / betrayal / self-betrayal. Maturity: childish / childlike / childishness posing as maturity (Big). Sex: Chinatown's negation is incest with the offspring of incest.
- Most TV crime stops at the Contradictory. Missing reaches tyranny. ...And Justice for All goes full circle back to positive.
- Comedy needs the end of the line too.
- "If a story does not reach the Negation of the Negation, it may strike the audience as satisfying, but never brilliant, never sublime." Greatness lies in the treatment of the negative side. When a story is weak, the forces of antagonism are weak; build the negative rather than making the protagonist more likable.
- Typical progression: Contrary in Act One, Contradictory later, Negation of the Negation in the last act (tragic, or back to the positive with a difference). Big jumps to the negation first; Casablanca opens there.

## Ch 15 Exposition
- Skill in exposition = making it invisible. Dramatize exposition: primary purpose is to further the immediate conflict; secondary is to convey information.
- No one tells someone what both already know unless it serves a stronger need. "Convert exposition to ammunition": characters use what they know as weapons in pursuit of what they want ("same hippie haircut... got you kicked out of school twenty years ago").
- Panning across photos on a mantel is telling, not showing.
- Parse exposition through the whole story, even into the climax. Two rules: never include what the audience can easily assume; never pass on exposition unless its absence would confuse. Interest comes from withholding information.
- Pace exposition: least important early, critical last. Critical exposition = secrets, painful truths characters don't want known.
- No "California scenes": strangers sharing deep secrets over coffee. Whatever is said hides what can't be said. Big revelations come only under maximum pressure (Evelyn, Vader).
- Create the desire to know before giving the information: make the audience ask "Why?"
- Lifelong spans need an unusually durable spine (Little Big Man, Last Emperor, Farewell My Concubine). Otherwise begin in medias res, as close to the climax as possible, so the protagonist has a long life of accumulated value to lose.
- Tell stories about people who have something to lose. Homeless alcoholic: nothing at stake, static portrait.
- "Table dusting": unmotivated exposition (Outbreak's "You don't know about Ebola, do you?").
- Scenes turn only on action or revelation. Powerful revelations come from Backstory saved for turning points. Revelations tend to hit harder; reserve them for act climaxes.
- Flashbacks: dramatize them as mini-dramas with their own turning points; only bring them in after creating the need to know (Casablanca's Paris; Reservoir Dogs).
- Dream sequences: "exposition in a ball gown." Montage: usually a lazy substitute for dramatization.
- Voice-over test: remove it; if the story still works, keep it (it's counterpoint). Telling narration throughout is lazy and patronizing.
- Study JFK for exposition management.

## Ch 16 Problems and Solutions
- Interest = curiosity (intellect: questions, "How will it turn out?") plus concern (emotion: pull toward positive values). The audience searches for the Center of Good and attaches to it. It must be at least in the protagonist; "good" is relative to a darker surrounding world (Godfather's loyalty, White Heat, The Night Porter, Lecter surrounded by worse people).
- Mystery (audience knows less: curiosity only; closed mystery needs 3+ suspects, open mystery needs multiple clues), Suspense (audience knows the same: curiosity plus concern, 90 percent of films), Dramatic Irony (audience knows more: concern only, dread and compassion; Sunset Boulevard, Betrayal). Mix them within suspense (Casablanca uses dramatic irony for Paris, mystery for Rick's decision). The axe-murderer corridor example shows all three.
- False Mystery: withholding what should be given to pad dull stretches (flash-forward plane crash then 30-minute flashback). Cheap Surprise: shock without insight (dream-fake death). True surprise is followed by insight.
- Coincidence: bring it in early and let it gain meaning (the shark stays and seems to choose evil); make it become a force of antagonism. Avoid coincidence after the midpoint. Never end on it: deus ex machina is a lie because we all must make our own meaning. Exception: antiplot, where coincidence means life is absurd.
- Comedy: dramatist admires humanity under pressure; comedy shows people screwing up in the best circumstances. Comedy writer is an angry idealist; ask "What am I angry about?" and attack an institution. Comedy can halt narrative drive for pure gags, tolerates coincidence and even deus ex machina if the protagonist suffered enormously and never despaired. Test: pitch the plot without jokes; if listeners laugh at each turn, it's a comedy. Turn method: "What's the opposite?", then "What's off-the-wall from that?"
- Point of view: within a scene (whose eyes; neutral distance invites laughter); within the story: staying with the protagonist's POV is harder and creates more empathy. More time with a character = more choices witnessed = more empathy.
- Adaptation: novel's strength is inner conflict, theatre's is personal conflict (dialogue), film's is extra-personal conflict (image). "THE PURER THE NOVEL, THE PURER THE PLAY, THE WORSE THE FILM." Choose works with conflict on all levels, weighted extra-personal. Method: read until saturated; reduce each event to one or two sentences; ask if it's well told (usually not, or too long); reorder chronologically; step outline; cut scenes and create new ones if needed; make the mental physical. "BE WILLING TO REINVENT." Keep the spirit. (Note: this conflicts with Leonard's "no added material" rule; see resolution in reference files.)
- Melodrama is under-motivation, not over-expression: lift motivation to match the size of the act.
- Holes: close if you can without a pointless scene; ask if the audience will notice (Chinatown's Ida Sessions); or expose and deny it (Ferrari's "Why I'm doing this I don't know"; Terminator's "you could go crazy thinking about this").

## Ch 17 Character
- The Mind Worm: the writer burrows into a character and designs the one event (Inciting Incident) that will push that particular person to his limit. Human nature is the subject that doesn't date (Faulkner, as cited by McKee).
- Characters are not human beings; they are works of art, clearer than real people.
- True character only through choice in dilemma. Key to true character is desire: what does he want now, soon, overall, knowingly, unknowingly?
- Motivation: understand it, but don't nail it to one cause (no case-study explanations, e.g. the child-abuse cliché); leave some mystery so the audience can fill it with its own experience. Edmund in Lear laughs at astrology as explanation. Once we see the deed, the why matters less.
- What others say about a character, and what he says about himself, are hints, not facts. Characters with lucid self-knowledge reciting self-explanatory dialogue are "boring but phony." Self-description must be validated or contradicted in action ("I stick my neck out for no man": not yet, Rick).
- Dimension = contradiction, either inside deep character (Macbeth: ambition vs guilt) or between characterization and deep character (charming thief). Must be consistent: a nice guy kicking a cat once is not a dimension. Traits and quirks (ex-con, finance expert, break dancer, karate, sax) are not dimensions.
- The protagonist must be the most dimensional character or the Center of Good shifts (Blade Runner: Batty steals empathy).
- Cast design: the protagonist creates the cast; each supporting role exists to bring out a different dimension of the protagonist. Supporting roles can have one or two dimensions (Terminator: machine vs human). Counterpoint between character and setting creates dimension. Bit parts: deliberately flat but not dull, one freshly observed trait; don't make them more interesting than you will use (the thieving cab driver creates false anticipation).
- Comic character: blind obsession ("humour") he doesn't see. When he sees it, comedy ends (Archie in Wanda becomes the romantic lead).
- Three tips: (1) Leave room for the actor: no stage directions of gestures and tones; the actor needs want, why, how, what stops him, consequences. Imagine ideal casting so you write less. Don't write lines the gesture already says ("Would you like this cup of coffee, darling?"). (2) Fall in love with all your characters, especially villains (Terminator's eyeball and vanity, the response menu). No one thinks they're bad (Lee Marvin anecdote as told by McKee); sociopaths are charming. Love without losing clear-headedness. (3) Character is self-knowledge: observation gives characterization; self-knowledge gives deep character. We are more alike than different, so the honest answer to "If I were this character..." is always human.

## Ch 18 The Text
- "Dialogue is not conversation." Real talk keeps the channel open; screen dialogue needs the swing of everyday talk with content above normal: compression and economy, direction (each exchange turns beats), purpose (each line builds to the turning point). Aristotle via McKee: speak as common people, think as wise men.
- Film is about 80 percent visual, 20 percent auditory (McKee's figure); theatre the reverse. Short, simply constructed sentences, noun-verb-object. Incomplete sentences are natural.
- Read aloud; avoid accidental rhymes. If a line feels particularly fine and literary, cut it.
- Short speeches (stichomythia). A page-long speech = a talking minute on one face. Off-screen voices lose half the comprehension (lip-reading) and lose the speaker's subtext. No monologues in life; break long speeches with the listener's reactions or parentheticals showing action/reaction (Amadeus).
- Suspense sentence: periodic sentence with meaning at the end. Cut trailing prepositional phrases.
- Silent screenplay: first ask how to play the scene with no dialogue (Bergman's waiter sniffs Anna; cut to the room). Law of diminishing returns for dialogue. Hitchcock (as quoted by McKee): "When the screenplay has been written and the dialogue has been added, we're ready to shoot."
- Description: write only what can be seen or heard. "He's been sitting there a long time" becomes "He stubs out his tenth cigarette". Present tense, vivid action. Name the thing: specific nouns and verbs, not adjective+noun or adverb+verb ("hammers a spike", "pads across the room"). Kill "there is / it is". Metaphor only if photographable ("In film, a tree is a tree", Forman via McKee); no "as if". No sarcastic asides to the reader. No "we see / we hear". No camera directions or CUT TO. Master scene format; suggest shots through paragraphing (each short paragraph implies a shot). Use INSERT and POV only when needed for comprehension.
- Image Systems: a category of imagery repeated with variation and subtlety, subliminal. Exclude 90 percent of reality; every object carries connotation. External imagery (flag = patriotism) is the student-film hallmark; internal imagery gives a category a meaning unique to this film (water as death in Les Diaboliques; prison bars and Rick-as-America and linking/separating in Casablanca; blind seeing in Chinatown; motherhood in Aliens; art as weapon in After Hours). Must stay unnoticed (Viridiana's rope gets "Symbol!"). Labeling symbols is vanity. Writer starts the image system; director and designers finish it.
- Titles name something solid: character, setting, theme, genre, ideally several (Jaws, Kramer vs. Kramer).

## Ch 19 A Writer's Method
- Outside-in (struggling): idea straight to scenes and dialogue; clinging to favorite scenes through rewrites; story never works.
- Inside-out (professional): roughly the first four of six months on cards. Step-outline: one or two sentences per scene, what happens and how it turns; on the back, its structural function (setup, inciting incident, act climaxes) for central plot and subplots. Purpose: to destroy work; 90 percent is mediocre. Meanwhile, side files fill with biographies, world, history, themes, images, idiom.
- Find the climax, rework backward.
- Pitch test: tell the story in ten minutes to an intelligent person, watch their eyes. If it doesn't hook, hold and move in ten minutes, it won't in 110. Go on only when most listeners respond with silent "Wow".
- Treatment: each scene expanded to a paragraph or more, present tense, moment-by-moment, with subtext (true thoughts and feelings), no dialogue. 60 to 90+ double-spaced pages for 40 to 60 scenes. Ten-page "treatments" are outlines. Rework until every moment lives in text and subtext.
- Screenplay from a full treatment: 5 to 10 pages a day; dialogue is best because characters have been silent so long; voices become character-specific. Problems found in draft get fixed back in the treatment (setups and payoffs), not by tinkering with lines.
- Premature dialogue chokes creativity and is the slowest path; you discover too late the idea doesn't work. Outline stage tells you in months, not years.
- Fade Out: the millipede fable; study thoughtfully, write boldly; courage to risk rejection.
