# Craft: what makes writing good

Each principle: what to do, the effect on the audience, the source. Load at step 2 (assessment) and before any drafting. Sources in brackets; quotes and labels in sources.md and research-notes/.

## Story

1. Every scene changes a value. Note the value and its charge at the start and end of each scene; if they match, cut or merge the scene. Effect: the audience feels time passing only when nothing changes; a turn resets attention. [McKee ch. 2, 11]

2. The gap between expectation and result is the engine. A character takes the smallest action he thinks will work; the world answers differently or harder; he must risk more. Effect: the audience, aligned with the character, is surprised with him; the "oh no / oh yes" moment is the same moment for both. [McKee ch. 7]

3. Escalate in kind, not in count. Each failed attempt removes that size of action forever; the next one must cost more will, more capacity, more risk. Effect: the audience can feel when the story repeats itself and stops leaning in. [McKee ch. 9; Raiders conference: "each cliffhanger is better than the one before"]

4. Know the climax before the scenes. Design the last act first and work backward; everything that doesn't serve it goes. Effect: endings decide whether the whole film is remembered as good ("movies are about their last twenty minutes"). [McKee ch. 5, 13, 19; Wilbur Smith: knows the ending; Stine: outlines every chapter]

5. Give the opposition more power than the hero. Map the negative side down to the Negation of the Negation of the central value. Effect: the win only means something if loss was likely; a weak opponent makes the ending feel rigged. [McKee ch. 14; Lucas, Raiders conference: "If we don't make him vulnerable, he's got no problems."]

6. Real choices are dilemmas. Put the protagonist between two goods or two evils of equal weight, built as a triangle. Effect: the audience can't predict the choice, so the choice reveals who he is. Good-vs-evil choices are predictable and reveal nothing. [McKee ch. 10, 13]

7. Stakes are measured against the character, not the universe. Ask: what is the worst that happens if he fails, and why is that unbearable for this person? Effect: audiences accept small worlds when the stake is total for the person in it. [McKee ch. 7; conflicts.md A.2]

8. Conflict on three levels at once. Inner (mind, body, emotion), personal (intimates), extra-personal (institutions, environment, time). Effect: complexity without a bigger cast; one scene can turn three values (the Kramer French toast scene). [McKee ch. 9]

9. Plant, then pay off with a second meaning. A setup should read one way the first time and another after the turn. Effect: the audience races back through the film and experiences the insight as its own discovery. [McKee ch. 10; Zemeckis on Back to the Future: "Everything is set up, everything is paid off."]

10. Show the mechanics early when the disaster is complex. Cameron's Titanic opens with an animation of how the ship sank, so the second half plays as experience, not explanation. Effect: when people understand the situation, they can fear the right things. [Titanic screenplay; Ron Howard: "To put people on the edge of their seats they have to fully understand what's going on."]

11. Withhold what you can. Never give information the audience can assume or doesn't yet want; create the question first. Effect: curiosity holds attention; answered questions release it. [McKee ch. 15; Spielberg on Jaws: suspense "without seeing the shark itself"]

12. Choose the audience's knowledge position on purpose. Mystery (knows less), suspense (same), dramatic irony (knows more). Effect: each produces a different emotion: curiosity, anxiety, dread. Kubrick on Barry Lyndon: telling the disaster in advance "gives away surprise but creates suspense." [McKee ch. 16; Kubrick, Ciment interview]

13. Alternate tension and release. Lower the pressure after each act climax, then top it. Shorten scenes and raise tempo approaching a climax; let the climax itself breathe. Effect: constant intensity numbs; contrast restores sensitivity. [McKee ch. 12; Stine: "intense" moments get "something funny to lighten up"]

14. Endings: inevitable and unexpected. Deliver the emotion the opening promised by a route nobody predicted; best if the climax holds one last turn. Effect: satisfaction plus surprise; the audience feels it could not have ended otherwise only after it ends. [McKee ch. 13 citing Aristotle and Goldman]

## Character

15. Empathy before likability. The audience must recognize a shared humanity in the protagonist; it does not have to like him. Hypocrisy and self-pity break the bond. [McKee ch. 7; conflicts.md A.1]

16. Two desires in conflict. A conscious goal he can name, and an unconscious one that contradicts it. Effect: the audience reads him better than he reads himself, which is the pleasure of watching people. [McKee ch. 7, 8]

17. Dimension is contradiction, not trivia. A consistent contradiction inside the character or between surface and core. A list of skills and quirks is not a character. [McKee ch. 17]

18. Competence is magnetic; vulnerability makes it matter. Lucas on Indy: "He's a real professional... that is the key to the whole thing", and in the same meeting, the need to make him vulnerable. Effect: we enjoy watching mastery and fear for it at once. [Raiders conference]

19. Every supporting role exists to show a different side of the protagonist. If two characters would react the same way to the same news, merge them. [McKee ch. 8, 17]

20. No one thinks he's the villain. Write antagonists from inside ("If I were him, what would I do?"); they do not act villainous because they want to get away with it. Effect: credible opposition, real fear. [McKee ch. 17]

21. Characters don't explain themselves. What a character says about himself is a claim the story tests by choice ("I stick my neck out for no man"). [McKee ch. 17; anti-slop.md]

## World and research

22. Know the world better than anyone. Clichés come from not knowing it. Research memory, imagination and fact before inventing events. Effect: the "telling detail" makes the audience trust the whole. [McKee ch. 3, 8; Kubrick: "Good research is an absolute necessity"; Wilbur Smith: "months and months of research where I live in the world I want to write about"]

23. Make it believable before making it grand. Kubrick: the starting point of any historical or future story "is to make you believe what you see." Blade Runner's world is built from retrofits, density and wear ("a messy patina"). Effect: belief is the precondition for every emotion. [Kubrick, Ciment; American Cinematographer 1982]

24. Small world, deep knowledge. A contained cast and few locations allow complete knowledge and complexity. [McKee ch. 3, 9]

25. Specific detail carries universal conflict. The conflict must need no insider knowledge; the texture should be as local as the story's real place. Effect: the audience discovers an unknown world and finds itself in it. [McKee intro; Leonard's belief #4]

## Writing process

26. Excavate before you outline. The writer's own memory is the only source of emotional truth. Ask what he lived, saw first, wants felt. [McKee ch. 3, 7, 17; Kaufman: "Say who you are"]

27. Outline, then let it surprise you. Step outline and treatment before dialogue; changes are cheap there. If the plot works out exactly as planned, the writer isn't listening to the story. [McKee ch. 6, 19; Stine; Cameron scriptments; King on found stories]

28. Write loose, revise hard. Drafting door closed, revision door open; second draft = first draft minus ten percent. [King; Bukowski "stay loose"; conflicts.md A.12]

29. Cut what you love if it serves nothing. "Kill your darlings" (King, quoting the tradition). The climax decides what stays. [King; McKee ch. 13]

30. Expect the first version to fail. Catmull: "all of our films start out as disasters"; first attempts reach a B; A comes from responding to what each attempt reveals. Review after every stage is built in for this reason. [Catmull, Salon 2014, McKinsey 2016]

## Medium

31. Play it without words first. Ask how each scene works with no dialogue; add words last. Garland reaches points where scripts "become dialogue-less"; McKee's Bergman waiter scene. Effect: images are processed before words and remembered longer. [McKee ch. 18; Garland]

32. Make the mental physical. Inner change must show in choice and action under pressure; declarations convince nobody. [McKee ch. 2, 8; Carpenter via McKee]

33. Keep technique invisible. Zemeckis: "I work really hard to try to keep film technique invisible." Symbols work only while unnoticed. [Zemeckis DGA 2013; McKee ch. 12, 18]

34. Style comes from the story. Boyle: find "the absolute style of the film from the story". Kaufman: what you do should be "specific to the medium". [Boyle; Kaufman]
