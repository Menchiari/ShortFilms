# Question bank

Load at step 1 (intake) and step 3 (question rounds).

## Rules
1. Ask only questions whose answer changes the story. If any answer leads to the same outline, don't ask.
2. Never ask what the user already answered, in this session or in the repo's files. Quote his answer back instead if relevant.
3. Order by dependency. Blocking questions first: whose story, what they want, what it costs, how it ends. Everything else waits.
4. Each question states what it decides, then gives 2 or 3 concrete options built from the user's own material, plus "your own". Options are [PROPOSED] and must not replace his idea; they explore it. Excavation questions about memory or feeling may give 2 or 3 prompts instead of options (what kind of answer helps), never a suggested answer.
5. Two kinds: CRAFT (design decisions) and EXCAVATION (pulling out what he already carries). Mix them; excavation answers often settle craft questions.
6. As many per round as the story needs. Mark each BLOCKING or LATER. Stop the round when the remaining questions depend on answers not yet given.
7. Questions are plain and short. No praise, no preamble, no "great question".
8. If Leonard's own words allow two readings (a fact or a belief? one action or two? which slot?), ask which he meant before offering options built on either.
9. Reference questions by number only inside the same file; the restatement must not point to a questions file that doesn't exist yet.
10. If a factual premise is uncertain (a law, a procedure, a date), say it is a research item and what changes depending on the answer. Never guess the fact. Every option that rests on an unverified fact carries the flag "(research item)".
11. Scope first: if a missing intake item (format, length) would remove what other questions decide (a short covering one day makes most second-act questions moot), it is BLOCKING and goes first.
12. Within the blocking set, keep the order: his own words with two readings, then whose story, what he wants (conscious and unconscious), what it costs, how it ends.
13. When a question is about Leonard's own sentence, option a) is always his sentence taken literally, with no theme added.
14. One axis per question. If two options can both be true, split the question.
15. Ending questions offer actions (what he does on screen), not outcomes, and cover up, down and ironic.
16. Excavation questions are never BLOCKING by default; they are asked early because they often settle craft questions.

## Format
```
Q[n] [BLOCKING|LATER] [CRAFT|EXCAVATION] <question>
Decides: <what in the story this settles>
a) <option, specific to this idea>
b) <option>
c) <option, if a third is useful>
d) Your own.
```

## Layer 0: intake (ask only what is missing)
- The idea, in any language.
- Length or format: micro short, short, medium, feature, series episode. Decides the act count and scene budget (structure.md).
- Vibe: the feeling and texture, in his words; references if any.
- Non-negotiables: what must stay, what must never happen.
- Working language and screenplay language. Default: answer in the language he writes in; ask about the screenplay language at step 6, not before.
- Whether the repo already holds material for this idea (notes, drafts, canon). Read it before asking anything.

## Layer 1: blocking questions
Whose story
- Who is the protagonist: single, plural (shared desire and fate), or multi (separate desires)? Decides spine and point of view.
- From whose point of view do we watch? Decides empathy and what the audience can know.
What they want
- Conscious desire: what would he answer if asked what he wants? Decides the spine in Archplot.
- Unconscious desire: what does he want without knowing, that contradicts the first? Decides depth and the climax.
- When does the want begin (inciting incident), and is it caused by him, by someone with power over him, or by chance?
What it costs
- The worst thing that happens if he fails, and why it is unbearable for him in particular. Decides stakes and the Negation of the Negation.
- What he must risk to try. Decides the dilemma.
How it ends
- Does he get it, not get it, or get it and lose something else? Decides the controlling idea and the ending's charge.
- The final image, if he has one.
- What is gained, what is lost (Leonard's belief #3).

## Layer 2: antagonism, world, genre
- Who or what opposes him at each level: inner, personal, extra-personal? What does each force want for itself?
- How far down the negative side does it go: Contrary, Contradictory, Negation of the Negation?
- Period and place: why this time and place? What does the period make possible or impossible? Is anything understandable only to insiders? (Leonard's belief #4)
- Genre or genre mix, and which conventions are being promised to the audience.
- Central Question in three layers: professional, personal, private.
- Formula letters C (what goes wrong at the midpoint) and D (what he must finally do).
- The story triangle: closed or open ending; external or internal emphasis; linear or not.

## Layer 3: later decisions
- Cast: who brings out which side of the protagonist? Stakes Character? Deflector? Ally?
- Subplots: does each contradict, echo, set up or complicate the main plot?
- Audience knowledge: mystery, suspense or dramatic irony at the major turns.
- Tone control: where the laughs are; how dark it may go.
- Image system: a category of images that repeats.
- Research list: facts the story depends on.
- Title: names something real in the story (McKee ch. 18). Never a tagline.
- Pipeline: if the repo has canon, shot list or prompt system, which bible fields feed which file.

## Excavation bank (adapt to the idea; ask 2 to 4 per round)
- What is the first image you saw when this idea came to you?
- Which parts of this did you live, see, or hear about from someone who did? Which parts are invented?
- What feeling should the audience walk out with? Describe it as a physical state if you can.
- Which moment of this story have you already imagined completely, with sound?
- Who is this really about? (A person, a version of yourself, someone you owe something to.)
- What detail about this world do you know that almost nobody else does?
- What would ruin this story for you if someone else made it?
- What are you angry about in this? (Comedy and satire need the target; McKee ch. 16.)
- What did the people in this world say to each other that you still remember word for word?
- What smell, sound, object or place belongs to this story and no other?
- If you've carried this idea for years: what has changed in it over time, and what never changed?
- What scares you about telling it?

## Question generators for weak spots found in the assessment
- Goal without stakes → "What exactly does he lose if he fails, and who sees him lose it?"
- Opponent is a role, not a person → "What does [opponent] want for himself, apart from stopping the protagonist?"
- Missing ending → "Pick the last thing he does on screen" with three options covering up, down and ironic.
- Trait as label (an illness, a type) → "Show me one ordinary day: where does [trait] cost him something?"
- Two different actions merged in the idea → name both and ask which is the story.
- Period possibly decorative → "What can't happen in this story if it moves to today?"
- Passive protagonist → "What does he do, on his own initiative, in the first ten minutes?"
