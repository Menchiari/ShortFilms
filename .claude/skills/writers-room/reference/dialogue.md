# Dialogue: subtext, voice, exposition

Load at step 6 (treatment and screenplay) and when the Braintrust flags dialogue.

## 1. Before writing a line
- Try the scene with no dialogue first (McKee ch. 18; Garland aims for scenes that "become dialogue-less"). Add words only where behavior can't carry the turn.
- Know, for each character in the scene: what he wants (an infinitive), what the other wants (directly opposed), what he won't say, and what he'd do if he could say it. That unspoken part is the subtext.
- Dialogue is written last because characters who have been silent through outline and treatment speak in their own voices (McKee ch. 19).

## 2. Subtext
- A scene must not be about what it seems to be about. If the words state the feeling, the actor has nothing to play (McKee ch. 11).
- Give the scene an activity that is not the subject: changing a tire while falling for each other; packing a suitcase while planning an escape.
- People mask in proportion to the stakes. The bigger the feeling, the more indirect the line. Confessions happen only under the pressure of two evils and still hide a deeper layer (Evelyn's confession in Chinatown is a plea for help).
- Narrators and characters alone also mask: they rationalize, flatter themselves, lie.
- Test: summarize the scene's subtext in one sentence. If that sentence appears in the dialogue, rewrite.

## 3. Voice
- Each character's speech comes from his biography: education, region, job, age, what he's afraid of. Two characters who would say the same line in the same words: one of them is redundant or underwritten (McKee ch. 8 cast polarization).
- Differentiate by: sentence length, vocabulary range, what they joke about, what they avoid naming, how they interrupt, whether they answer questions.
- Not everyone is articulate. Most people under pressure say less, repeat themselves, go off topic, answer a different question, or say nothing.
- Regional and period speech is researched, not invented: transcripts, recordings, people who lived it. For Italian scripts, record register choices (tu/Lei, dialect, swearing) in the story bible per character.
- Let characters be wrong, unfair, funny, or boring in their own way.

## 4. Exposition
- Convert exposition to ammunition: facts come out as weapons in a fight for something (McKee ch. 15).
- Nobody tells someone what both already know unless it hurts.
- Never explain what the audience can assume; never withhold what would confuse.
- Create the question before the answer.
- Save secrets for turning points.
- No "As you know", no new arrival who needs everything explained, no character summarizing the plot so far.

## 5. Shape on the page
- Short speeches; rapid exchange. A long speech must be broken by the listener's reactions or the speaker's reaction to himself (McKee ch. 18, Amadeus example).
- Meaning at the end of the sentence (the "suspense sentence"); cut trailing prepositional tails.
- Incomplete sentences, interruptions, silences are normal.
- Read aloud; cut accidental rhymes and tongue-twisters; cut any line that sounds written.
- Don't write lines the gesture already says ("Would you like this cup of coffee, darling?" while handing the cup).
- No parentheticals telling the actor how to feel. Use them only for action or a reaction that changes the line.
- Interruptions in Fountain: end the cut-off line with a double hyphen (--). Never use the em dash character.
- Ellipsis (...) only for trailing off, not for drama.

## 6. Quick fixes
- On-the-nose line → replace with an action that shows it, or a line about something else that carries it.
- Speech explaining a feeling → cut; show the choice the feeling causes.
- Everyone eloquent → lower the vocabulary of the people who would have less; let someone fail to find the word.
- Witty exchange that stops the story → keep only if it also turns the value of the scene.
- Theme spoken aloud → delete; check that the climax proves it without words.
- Aphorism at scene end → end on an action, a reaction, or an unanswered question instead (anti-slop.md).
