# Complications library (RPG catalog translated to film)

Source: Narrative_Structure.xlsx, a tabletop adventure-design catalog. Use it as a parts bin when a story needs a complication, villain type, climax shape or twist. It is not a structural authority. Each entry: the film version, the effect on the audience, where it usually sits, and the McKee check it must pass.

General rules for using this file
- A complication must come from the story's world and characters (McKee: setting limits what can happen). If it could be dropped into any film, it's a cliché.
- Every complication must pass a point of no return: after it, the previous kind of action can't work anymore.
- Prefer complications that hit two or three levels at once (inner, personal, extra-personal).
- Label any complication Claude Code proposes as [PROPOSED].

## Story engines (catalog: Theme, Goal)
- Task with obstacles (Action/Adventure): a concrete objective, obstacles between. Effect: clear rooting interest. Check: obstacles must escalate in kind.
- Revenge: a wrong so personal the hero can't let it live; ends in a showdown. Effect: moral tension about how far is too far. Check: give the target a human side or the story turns into a shooting gallery.
- Mystery: find out who did what. Effect: curiosity only; audience knows less. Check: closed mystery needs at least three credible suspects; open mystery needs multiple clues and one hidden flaw (McKee ch. 16).
- Thwart the plan: learn of an opponent's plan, gather allies and means, stop it. Effect: a ticking structure. Check: the plan must be smart enough to nearly work.
- Survive the environment: cross a hostile place. Effect: physical suspense, nature as antagonist. Check: give nature a personality through repetition (Jaws' shark) or it becomes scenery.
- Escape: captured early; learn the place, the routine, the people; plan; execute. Effect: process suspense, small victories. Check: the plan must break and be improvised at the climax.

## Hooks (catalog: Story Hook)
- Grim necessity: if he doesn't act, he suffers or dies. Film use: the inciting incident threatens something the protagonist can't walk away from. Check: say exactly what he loses.
- Pressing buttons: the antagonist's side effect hits the hero's personal goal, or does the thing he hates most. Film use: makes an impersonal conflict personal (McKee's crime convention "make it personal"). Check: avoid the cliché versions (the partner killed, the family threatened) unless reinvented.

## Plot shapes (catalog: Plot)
- A-B-C Quest: to get A he must first get B; to get B he needs C... until he can return to A. Film use: Leonard's belief #2; progressive complications as prerequisites. Effect: the goal recedes as the effort grows. Check: each detour must change the hero or raise the stakes, not just add errands.
- Event backdrop: the opponent's move happens during a public event (wedding, funeral, festival, match). Effect: free tension, crowds, fixed clock. Check: the event must matter to someone in the story.
- Accumulation: collect pieces (clues, evidence, allies, money) across places. Effect: visible progress bar. Check: collecting must cost something each time.
- Series of opponents: a sequence of distinct adversaries, each a life-or-death duel. Effect: episodic but rising. Check: McKee warns of repetition; each opponent must test a different weakness.

## Climax shapes (catalog: Climax)
- Natural or divine consequence: the antagonist's own tampering unleashes a disaster the hero must escape. Check: never let the disaster resolve the story by itself (deus ex machina is banned, McKee ch. 16); the hero must still choose and act.
- Prevented deed: the hero, beaten and believed dead, arrives at the decisive moment of the opponent's plan. Effect: reversal from lowest point. Check: plant the plan and the hero's knowledge of it earlier.
- Scattered duels: the group splits; each member faces the enemy he most deserves. Effect: multiple payoffs at once (McKee: climax subplots inside the main climax). Check: each duel pays off something planted.
- Chase to ground: hero chases antagonist to his goal; antagonist chases hero during the final act; or the hidden antagonist bolts when exposed. Effect: kinetic climax. Check: terrain must matter.
- Mass battle: armies clash, hero cuts toward the leader. Check: keep a personal objective visible inside the chaos.
- Throne-room duel: the group faces the antagonist together. Check: who lands the decisive act must be the protagonist, by choice.

## Settings that generate conflict (catalog: General and Specific Setting)
- On the road; at sea; underground; another world; big city; the hero's home town (family and old friends drawn in); a lost place; a laboratory; a workshop or guild quarter. Film rule: pick the setting whose rules create the story's obstacles (McKee ch. 3). The hero's home town is the most useful here because it brings personal conflict for free.

## Antagonist types (catalog: Master Villain, Minor Villain, Monster)
- Chance (no villain; or a trickster): a string of unrelated accidents. Film use: comedy or antiplot only; in drama coincidence must become meaningful early (McKee ch. 16).
- Avenger: wants to punish a real or imagined wrong; partly sympathetic. Effect: moral ambiguity. Check: let him be partly right.
- Organizer: runs a criminal or institutional network; reasons only by profit. Effect: systemic threat; can be negotiated with when it pays.
- Conqueror: takes territory; armies. Effect: scale; risk of abstraction.
- Corruptor: turns good things bad; can target one person (a mentor who corrupts) or a whole place. Effect: the threat is to the hero's soul, not his body; strong for inner stories.
- Destroyer: destroys for its own sake. Check: weakest dramatically unless given a reason the audience can understand.
- Corrupted hero: a former good figure turned servant of the antagonist. Effect: the hero sees his possible future.
- Interrogator: the cruel specialist. Check: avoid sadism as decoration.
- Sniveling adviser: the cowardly, brilliant counsel at the throne. Film use: comic menace, information source.
- Ravager: a beast with no plan. Film use: survival stories; give it behavior and rules.

## Allies, witnesses, red herrings
- The witness who saw too much (catalog: gibbering madman): knows part of the truth, can't say it clearly, must be kept close. Effect: clues delivered under pressure.
- Lying rumor: an interesting false fact about the antagonist. Effect: misdirection the audience shares. Check: when it collapses, it should reveal something true.
- False path: the prize already taken. Effect: reversal, new direction.
- Fake expert: an ally who turns out to know nothing. Effect: comic or tragic waste of time; raises the clock.
- Extraneous details: too many facts, some irrelevant. Check: in film, this reads as noise unless the audience knows it's being misled.
- The tool that doesn't work: the long-sought weapon or method fails at the moment of use. Effect: forces a human solution at the climax (McKee's "crisis within the climax").

## Traps (catalog: Deathtrap)
- Framed: accused of what he didn't do; must escape the authorities. Effect: everyone becomes an opponent.
- Rock and a hard place: the obvious exit leads to worse danger. Effect: dilemma under time pressure; escalation by design.

## Chases
- Wrong terrain: a chase in a place unsuited to it (horses in a palace, cars in a market). Effect: novelty within a convention.
- Foot chase: through streets, roofs, corridors. Check: give the pursued a destination and the pursuer a reason.

## Omens and prophecy (translate into setups)
- A portrait of a lookalike / a repeated past choice: the hero faces the same choice someone made before him. Film use: backstory as mirror.
- A prediction of doom: a warning, then a calamity that points to the antagonist. Check: in realistic genres, use a human warning, not a prophecy.
- The hero fulfills a prophecy: in realism, translate to "the role others expect him to play".
- Comet or sign: a public image that becomes symbolic. Check: never label it (McKee's image systems).
- Animal duel as rehearsal: a parallel event that teaches the hero how to win. Film use: a planted third-act solution.

## Secret weaknesses
- Ignorance of human behavior: the antagonist misjudges what people will sacrifice. Effect: the hero's humanity becomes the weapon.
- An element he hides from: a weakness he disguises inside another order. Effect: discoverable clue.
- Love: the antagonist loves someone; that person is his lever. Effect: humanizes and endangers.

## Special conditions (constraints that create invention)
- No weapons: the hero must enter disarmed. Effect: ingenuity.
- Time limit: the plan concludes at a fixed time. Effect: everything costs time.
- The usual skill doesn't work here. Effect: the protagonist must grow.
- A curse that worsens daily: a decline the hero sees in the mirror. Film use: illness, exhaustion, addiction, debt.
- Stolen identity: no one recognizes him. Effect: isolation.
- Can't hurt the opponent: the antagonist hides inside someone who must be protected. Effect: dilemma.
- A watcher who comments: a powerful figure who sees everything and berates. Film use: a parent, a boss, a teacher.
- No law-breaking: the hero must stay legal at the moment it would help most. Effect: dilemma between goal and standing.

## Moral quandaries (catalog: Moral Quandary; McKee: dilemma)
- Save people or hurt the enemy.
- Honor: winning requires breaking the hero's own code.
- Two needed allies who hate each other.
- Two allies demand opposite strategies; choosing one loses the other.
All four are McKee triangles: choosing one good sacrifices another.

## Cruel tricks (twists)
- Ally turns traitor. Check: plant the motive so it reads as inevitable afterward (McKee: logic is retroactive).
- The mission is a ruse: the hero has been working for the antagonist. Effect: the whole story re-reads.
- Wanted by the law: limited resources, must move in secret.
- Forced to work for the villain: a hostage or hold over the hero.
- The villain travels with the group: learning their plans; mystery of which companion it is.
