# McKee, Story (1997), distilled

Paraphrase of the whole book, chapter by chapter, written as working rules. McKee is the default authority in this system (see conflicts.md). Page-level notes: research-notes/02-mckee-notes.md.

## Introduction: principles, not rules
- A rule says "do it this way". A principle says "this has always worked". Follow principles; mastery of form comes before breaking it.
- Archetype vs stereotype: a story travels when a universal human conflict is dressed in specific, never-before-seen cultural detail. It stays home when narrow, culture-bound experience is dressed in generic detail. Use this as the test of Leonard's universality belief.
- Originality is distinctive content plus distinctive shaping, never eccentricity for its own sake.
- Writers fail when they want to prove an idea or vent an emotion; they succeed when they want to move an audience. The audience is smarter than most films; stay ahead of it.

## 1. The Story Problem
- Story design is at least three quarters of the work. Core questions: who, what they want, why, how they try, what stops them, what it costs.
- Two failure modes: the understructured slice of life ("but it actually happened" is the weakest reason to include anything) and the overstructured spectacle (motion mistaken for meaning).
- Story is a metaphor for life: abstract to the essence without losing life as lived.
- Story talent (turning life into meaningful event) outranks literary talent (beautiful language). Trivial material told brilliantly beats profound material told badly.

## 2. The Structure Spectrum
- Structure = events selected from the characters' lives, arranged to create specific emotion and express a view of life.
- Story event = meaningful change in a character's situation, expressed as a value, achieved through conflict. Coincidental change is not a story event.
- Values: binary qualities (alive/dead, love/hate, truth/lie, hope/despair) that can flip.
- Scene: action through conflict in roughly continuous time and place that turns at least one value. Test: value charge at the start vs the end. No change = nonevent; usually exposition; cut it and move the information. A feature has 40 to 60 scenes.
- Beat: one action/reaction exchange. Sequence: 2 to 5 scenes ending in a bigger turn. Act: sequences ending in a major reversal. Story: acts building to an absolute, irreversible change.
- Every line of dialogue or description either turns behavior or sets up the conditions for a turn.
- Story triangle:
  - Archplot (classical design): active protagonist, mainly external conflict, causality, continuous time, consistent reality, closed ending, irreversible change.
  - Miniplot: reduces the classical (open ending, inner conflict, multiple or passive protagonists). Still a story.
  - Antiplot: reverses it (coincidence, nonlinear time, inconsistent reality).
  - Below the triangle, Nonplot: nothing changes; portrait, not story.
- Seven spectrums: closed/open ending, external/internal conflict, single/multiple protagonist, active/passive protagonist, linear/nonlinear time, causality/coincidence, consistent/inconsistent reality. Most films sit between corners.
- The audience shrinks as a story moves away from the Archplot because most people experience their own lives as Archplot. Budget must follow audience size.
- Miniplot and Antiplot are reactions to classical form; masters learned the classical first. Breaking form to look "not Hollywood" is posturing. Write only what you believe.

## 3. Structure and Setting
- Clichés come from not knowing the story's world.
- Setting has four dimensions: period, duration, location, level of conflict.
- Every world has its own laws of probability; once the audience learns them, breaking them feels like betrayal.
- There is no portable story: an honest story belongs to one place and time.
- Keep the world small enough to know completely.
- Research three ways: memory (relive it and write it down), imagination (live a day of the character), fact (library). Research feeds events; it must not become procrastination.
- Creativity is choice: invent five to twenty versions of a scene, keep the one truest to character and world that hasn't been seen. If the cliché location is right, invent variations inside it.

## 4. Structure and Genre
- The audience arrives knowing genre conventions. Fulfil them, but by an unexpected route.
- McKee's genre list: Love Story, Horror, Modern Epic, Western, War, Maturation, Redemption, Punitive, Testing (willpower vs temptation), Education (worldview turns positive), Disillusionment (worldview turns negative), Comedy, Crime (subgenre set by whose point of view), Social Drama, Action/Adventure; supra-genres Historical, Biography, Docudrama, Mockumentary, Musical, Sci-Fi, Sports, Fantasy, Animation, Art Film.
- Conventions are the rhyme scheme: keep the convention, avoid the cliché inside it.
- Genre study: list similar works (including failures), break each into setting, role, event, value, compare, find what the genre always does.
- Genres evolve with society; antique values bore audiences.
- Write in the genre you love; a screenplay takes six months to a year or more.

## 5. Structure and Character
- Structure and character are the same thing seen from two sides.
- Characterization = observable traits. True character = choices under pressure; more pressure, truer choice.
- Principal characters must be at heart different from their surface; the best stories also change that inner nature.
- Structure applies rising pressure; characterization makes the choices credible. Change an event and you change the character.
- Spend most of the design effort on the last-act climax. If characterization makes the climax incredible, change the characterization, keep the deep character.

## 6. Structure and Meaning
- Story fuses idea and emotion in the same moment (aesthetic emotion); life delivers them separately.
- Premise: the question or image that started the writing. It can be dropped if the story turns elsewhere.
- Story proves an idea through events, without explanation. Dialogue is not the author's lectern.
- Controlling Idea: one sentence = value at the climax + the main cause. Find it from the climax, not before. Too many ideas cancel out.
- Progression swings between Idea and Counter-Idea; at the crisis they collide and one wins.
- Give the opposing side full force. A weak opposition makes the ending meaningless and the film preachy.
- Endings: idealist (up), pessimist (down), ironic (both). Irony is a clear statement of what is gained and what is lost, not a blur, not luck. Irony endures longest and is the hardest to write.
- The writer's only duty is to tell the truth: check that you believe the controlling idea.

## 7. The Substance of Story
- Protagonist: willful; has a conscious desire; often an unconscious desire that contradicts it; has the capacity to pursue it; has at least a chance; can go to the end of the line.
- Empathy is required, sympathy optional. Empathy = the audience recognizes shared humanity and roots for the desire. Hypocrisy and self-pity break the bond.
- Every character takes the minimum, safe action that he thinks will work. In story the world reacts differently or more strongly: the gap between expectation and result. The character must then take a harder, riskier action.
- Risk test: what is the worst thing that happens if he fails? If the answer is "life goes back to normal", there is no story. The value of a desire equals the risk taken for it.
- Three levels of conflict: inner, personal, extra-personal.
- Write from the inside: ask "If I were this character in these circumstances, what would I do?", then step out and ask "What is the opposite of that?"
- Write the reactions: the audience knows genre actions in advance; surprise lives in how people react.

## 8. The Inciting Incident
- Design the world: work, politics (power, including inside families and love), rituals, values, biographies, backstory.
- Cast by polarization: no two characters react the same way to the same event; if two do, merge or cut.
- Authenticity = internal consistency, built from a few telling details.
- The inciting incident radically upsets the balance of the protagonist's life, raises the desire to restore it, defines the object of desire, starts the quest, raises the major dramatic question and plants the image of the obligatory scene (the confrontation the audience now expects).
- It must happen on screen. Place it as soon as the audience knows enough to feel it fully, usually in the first quarter.
- To find it: the worst thing that could happen and how it becomes the best, or the best and how it becomes the worst.

## 9. Act Design
- Progressive complications pass points of no return: once a level of action fails, it can't be repeated. A sagging second act recycles first-act actions.
- Nothing moves forward except through conflict.
- Complication = conflict on one level. Complexity = conflict on all three levels at once (the Kramer French toast scene turns three values with almost no dialogue). Prefer a contained cast and few locations with complexity.
- Feature minimum: three major reversals. Act One about a quarter; last act short and accelerating.
- Long second act: add subplots or a mid-act climax. More acts risk cliché and numbness.
- The last two act climaxes must have opposite charges.
- A subplot must contradict, echo, set up or complicate the main plot; otherwise it splits the film.
- "Free to break convention, but only to put something more important in its place" (paraphrase of the Kiss of the Spider Woman example).

## 10. Scene Design
- Each scene objective serves the spine. Ideal: every scene is a turning point.
- Turning points give surprise, curiosity, insight (the audience races back through the story to find the planted answer) and a new direction.
- Self-expression lives in how you turn the story, not in the language.
- Setups must read one way first and another way after the turn. Too subtle: missed. Too heavy: predicted.
- Logic is retroactive: think the unthinkable, then go back and plant it.
- Emotion comes from a change of value in a character we empathize with. Repeating the same emotion halves it, then reverses it.
- True choice is a dilemma (two goods or two evils), built on a triangle, not a seesaw.

## 11. Scene Analysis
- When a scene fails, look at the action under the words, not the words.
- If a scene is about what it seems to be about, it is on the nose. Give actors a subtext.
- Five steps: define conflict (each side's want as an infinitive), opening value, beats named as emotive gerunds, closing value, locate the turning point.

## 12. Composition
- Unity: the inciting incident should make the climax feel inevitable. Within unity, maximum variety.
- Pace like breathing: tension, release, higher tension.
- Rhythm = scene length (average 2 to 3 minutes, varied). Tempo = activity inside the scene. Shorten scenes and raise tempo approaching a climax, then let the climax itself breathe.
- Express progression socially (spread outward), personally (drive inward), symbolically (images grow toward archetype, never labeled), ironically.
- Transitions hinge on something shared or opposed between two scenes.

## 13. Crisis, Climax, Resolution
- Crisis: the final dilemma, facing the strongest antagonism; keep it static and on screen.
- Climax: an absolute change whose meaning moves the audience, clear without explanation. Best if it holds one more turn.
- Rewrite backward from the climax; cut anything the ending doesn't need.
- Give the audience the emotion promised, by a route it didn't expect: inevitable and unexpected.
- Resolution: settle leftover subplots, show the spread of effects, give a slow curtain.

## 14. The Principle of Antagonism
- The protagonist is only as compelling as the forces against him. Weak story = weak antagonism; strengthen the negative instead of decorating the hero.
- Map the negative side: Contrary, Contradictory, Negation of the Negation (a qualitatively worse state: tyranny for justice, self-hate for love, self-deception for truth, self-enslavement for freedom). Great stories reach the last one.

## 15. Exposition
- Make exposition invisible. Convert it to ammunition: characters use facts as weapons to get what they want.
- Never tell what the audience can assume; never withhold what would confuse.
- Pace exposition; save secrets for the turning points they create. Create the desire to know before answering.
- Begin as close to the climax as possible; tell stories about people with something to lose.
- Flashbacks must be dramatized and wanted. Dream sequences and montages usually hide undramatized information. Voice-over must survive the removal test.

## 16. Problems and Solutions
- Interest = curiosity + concern. The audience finds the Center of Good and attaches to it, even in criminals, by contrast with a worse world.
- Mystery (audience knows less), Suspense (same), Dramatic Irony (more). Avoid false mystery and cheap surprise.
- Coincidence early can become meaningful; never resolve an ending by coincidence.
- Comedy attacks an institution; the comic character has a blind obsession.
- Staying in the protagonist's point of view is harder and builds more empathy.
- Adaptation: film is strongest in external conflict; reduce, reorder, externalize. (Leonard limits this: see conflicts.md.)
- Melodrama is under-motivation, not overstatement. Fix holes, or admit and wave them through.

## 17. Character
- Characters are designed artworks, clearer than people.
- Desire is the key; motivation should be understood but not reduced to one cause.
- What a character says about himself is evidence to be tested by choice.
- Dimension = consistent contradiction. The protagonist must be the most dimensional character.
- The protagonist generates the cast; each role exists to bring out one of his dimensions. Bit parts: one fresh trait, no more than will be used.
- Leave room for the actor; love every character, especially villains (no one thinks he is bad); character comes from self-knowledge.

## 18. The Text
- Dialogue is not conversation: compress, give it direction and purpose, keep it sounding spoken. Short sentences, short speeches, meaning at the end of the sentence.
- First try to play every scene without dialogue.
- Description: present tense, only what can be seen or heard, precise nouns and verbs, no "we see", no camera directions, paragraphs that imply shots.
- Image systems: a category of images repeated with variation, never pointed at.
- Titles name something real in the story.

## 19. A Writer's Method
- Work from the inside out: step outline on cards, months of it, throwing most away; climax found, then work backward; pitch the story in ten minutes and watch the listener; only then treatment (60 to 90 pages, every moment with subtext, no dialogue); only then screenplay.
- Premature dialogue locks the writer into scenes he loves and hides that the story doesn't work.
