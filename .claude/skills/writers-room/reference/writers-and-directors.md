# Writers and directors: mechanisms and lenses

Use a lens only when it fits the project's vibe or the user names it. Each entry: repeatable mechanisms (what the work does, scene by scene), a lens question set for review, and what to study. "Observable" means the mechanism can be checked by watching the film; quotes carry their labels in research-notes/04.

Rule: lenses are for diagnosis. Never imitate a famous scene; borrow the mechanism, not the content.

## Stephen King
Mechanisms
- Situation first, characters second, plot found by digging ("found things, like fossils"). Start from a what-if pressure on ordinary people.
- Draft fast with the door closed; revise with the door open. Second draft = first minus ten percent.
- Pacing by omission: leave out the parts readers skip (Elmore Leonard's rule, as King tells it).
- Description: a few well-chosen details; thin description disorients, heavy description buries.
- Write for one Ideal Reader.
Lens questions: Is the situation strong enough that the characters would generate plot on their own? What can be cut by ten percent without loss? Which details are doing the work of ten?
Study: On Writing; Misery (pressure in one room); The Body / Stand by Me (memory and childhood told without nostalgia).

## Charles Bukowski
Mechanisms
- Write from what was lived, plainly; refuse the poetic lie ("lie to yourself in a poem in order to simply make a poem, that you fail").
- Short declarative sentences; humor out of misery; no decoration.
- Endurance over inspiration: write tonight.
Lens questions: Where does the draft pretend? Which sentence was written to sound like writing? Where is the shame or the joke that a real person would include and a careful writer would cut?
Study: Ham on Rye (adolescence, humiliation, no self-pity); Factotum; Barfly (his own screenplay).

## R. L. Stine
Mechanisms
- Title first; the title suggests the plot.
- Full chapter-by-chapter outline before writing, including how each chapter ends; writing is then fast.
- Every chapter ends on an open question.
- After intensity, a laugh; scary and funny together.
Lens questions: Does every scene end on a turn that opens a question? Is there relief after each peak? Can the story be outlined to its last chapter before drafting?
Study: any Goosebumps book read with a pencil marking chapter endings; his NPR and Parade interviews.

## Wilbur Smith
Mechanisms
- Months of research and living in the world before starting; start only when the opening is clear.
- Knows the ending; lets characters find the route.
- Characters drawn from real people, enlarged; family sagas so characters carry history between books.
- Regular working hours; momentum by habit.
Lens questions: Does the physical world (landscape, weapons, work, animals, weather) carry danger by itself? Do the characters feel larger than life but anchored in someone real? Is the opening certain?
Study: When the Lion Feeds; River God; his memoir On Leopard Rock.

## Steven Spielberg (early films)
Mechanisms
- Hide the threat; show its effect (Jaws: the broken shark forced suspense "without seeing the shark itself"; Duel: the driver is never shown; observable).
- Give the hero a personal weakness the climax forces him to face (Brody's fear of the water; observable).
- Hero = competence + vulnerability; the villain is a rival who mirrors him (Raiders conference).
- Escalating set pieces: each one better than the last, each escape plausible (Raiders conference).
- The romantic lead has her own agenda and agency, not decoration (Raiders conference on Marion).
- Ordinary people in extraordinary danger; humor inside danger.
Spielberg (historical films)
- One concrete human detail stands for the incomprehensible number (the red coat, Schindler's List; "as obvious as a little girl in red").
- Make the audience present, not informed: Saving Private Ryan's newsreel look, handheld for about 90 percent, low shutter angles, "ugly lighting" (Kaminski, AC 1998).
- A mission frame that turns a historical event into a personal question (Ryan: is one life worth eight; observable).
- Procedure as suspense: a count, a deal, a negotiation (Lincoln's vote count, Bridge of Spies' exchange; observable).
- A repeated line becomes a character signature and a theme without stating it (Bridge of Spies, "Would it help?"; observable).
- Violence planned as complicated set pieces with innocents at risk (Munich; observable).
Lens questions: What is withheld, and is its effect shown instead? What single human detail carries the scale? Does each set piece top the last and get out plausibly? Is the hero's flaw tested at the climax?
Study: Duel, Jaws, Close Encounters, Raiders, E.T.; Empire of the Sun, Schindler's List, Saving Private Ryan, Munich, Lincoln, Bridge of Spies. Read the Raiders story conference transcript (1978).

## Stanley Kubrick
Mechanisms
- Research until the world is believable ("Good research is an absolute necessity"; a year of preparation on Barry Lyndon).
- Choose the audience's knowledge on purpose: announce outcomes to create suspense and irony ("not what is going to happen, but how it will happen").
- Narration only for economy and irony, never to explain feelings.
- Build around a handful of strong, self-contained sequences first; connections later (the "non-submersible units", reported by Aldiss).
- Research the counter-idea in human nature instead of preaching (McKee on Paths of Glory, Dr. Strangelove, Full Metal Jacket).
- Treat the plan as a discovery process ("as much a matter of discovery as it is the execution of a plan").
Lens questions: What does the audience know at each point, and is that a choice? Does the story argue the opposing view at full strength? Which five or six sequences carry the film, and are they each unsinkable?
Study: Paths of Glory, Dr. Strangelove, 2001, A Clockwork Orange, Barry Lyndon, The Shining, Full Metal Jacket; the Ciment interviews.

## Ridley Scott
Mechanisms
- Worlds built by accumulation and wear: retrofits, density, a "messy patina", everyday objects designed for the period (AC 1982).
- The setting expresses class and economy (Alien's crew argue about money and contracts; McKee's analysis).
- Seriousness: the story touches its big idea without lecturing ("It's not a film about genetics. It touches on it").
Lens questions: Is the world used, dirty, layered by history? Do characters talk about work, money and fatigue like real workers? Is the big idea present without being explained?
Study: Alien, Blade Runner, Thelma & Louise, Gladiator, The Duellists, Black Hawk Down.

## Danny Boyle
Mechanisms
- Open by bursting through the door: energy and the film's identity in the first minutes, little homework for the audience ("I like films to be visceral").
- Style derived from the story each time; change approach every film.
- Subjectivity: camera and music put the audience inside the character's state.
- Heavy rehearsal; "Thou shalt not bore" (quoting Wilder).
Lens questions: Does the opening announce the film's world and rhythm? Is the style a consequence of this story, or decoration? Where is the audience inside the character's head?
Study: Shallow Grave, Trainspotting, 28 Days Later, Sunshine (written by Garland), Slumdog Millionaire, 127 Hours, Steve Jobs.

## James Cameron
Mechanisms
- Scriptment before script: a long, present-tense prose telling with dialogue embedded, so the whole film exists before formatting.
- Teach the mechanics before the disaster (Titanic's sinking animation), then live it.
- Escalation by stacking and reversing: each victory reveals a bigger threat; false endings in action (McKee on The Terminator's double false ending).
- Rules of the threat set early and kept (Terminator's capabilities; observable).
- Love the villain enough to give it surprising behavior (McKee on the Terminator's eye repair and response menu).
- A domestic core under spectacle (Cameron on Avatar 2: "the family's your fortress"; his bar: heart, mind, imagination).
Lens questions: Does the audience understand the rules and geography before the action? Does each act end by revealing a bigger threat? Does the spectacle sit on a simple human bond?
Study: The Terminator, Aliens, The Abyss, Terminator 2, Titanic. Read a Cameron scriptment next to its final film.

## Ron Howard
Mechanisms
- "every story should be a suspense story"; even drama runs on a question.
- "all stories are really about coping": ordinary people handling pressure, often with humor.
- Explain the situation clearly so tension can work (Apollo 13).
- Ensemble warmth: secondary characters given room and spontaneity.
Lens questions: What is the suspense question in every scene? Do people cope in human ways (jokes, irritation, small kindnesses) under pressure? Is the technical situation clear enough to fear for?
Study: Parenthood, Apollo 13, Cocoon, Rush, Thirteen Lives.

## Robert Zemeckis (with Bob Gale)
Mechanisms
- Density of setups and payoffs: "Everything is set up, everything is paid off" (Back to the Future).
- Rewrite many times; scenes improve through repeated drafts (Gale).
- Jokes grounded in what the character would know from his own world (Gale).
- Character-driven episodic structure when the character is strong enough (Zemeckis on Forrest Gump).
- Technique kept invisible.
Lens questions: Is every planted object, line and place used again? Does each payoff also set up something new? Is warmth earned by the characters' decency under pressure rather than declared?
Study: Back to the Future (the annotated screenplay), Used Cars, Romancing the Stone, Who Framed Roger Rabbit, Forrest Gump, Cast Away.

## Charlie Kaufman
Mechanisms
- Write honestly about yourself and your confusion ("Say who you are").
- Refuse inherited templates; let the form come from the subject ("There's no template").
- Use the medium for what only it can do; films resemble dreams in how they handle inner life.
- Allow the script to change as you discover; "Do not simplify."
- Treat failure as proof of risk.
Lens questions: Where is the writer hiding? What form does this specific subject demand? What has been simplified that should stay complex?
Study: Being John Malkovich, Adaptation, Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind, Synecdoche, New York, Anomalisa; the 2011 BAFTA lecture.

## Alex Garland
Mechanisms
- Meaning "by inference, not by statement"; the audience must participate.
- Aim for scenes that "become dialogue-less".
- Contained settings and small casts around a big idea (observable in Ex Machina).
- Exposition only when motivated by a character's truth.
- Hide the hand: don't telegraph the turn ("I was trying to not show my hand").
- Write the thing you're obsessed with.
Lens questions: What does the audience infer that no one says? Can this scene lose its dialogue? Is the idea present in situations, not speeches?
Study: 28 Days Later, Sunshine, Never Let Me Go (screenplay), Ex Machina, Annihilation, Civil War.

## Ed Catmull and the Braintrust (review culture, not a style)
- The Braintrust "had no authority"; it could not tell the director what to do. Notes are diagnosis; the filmmaker decides. Candor is required; people hold back because they "don't want to embarrass themselves".
- Composed of filmmakers. Power removed from the room.
- Every film starts as a disaster; quality comes from responding to each version's problems.
- No template: Disney's formula years ("a musical with five to seven songs and a funny sidekick") are his warning.
- What Leonard heard: I did not find Catmull defending classical structure. The verified record warns against templates. Leonard's own position (break structure knowing why) is closer to Catmull than he expected.
Used in: review.md and agents/braintrust.md.
