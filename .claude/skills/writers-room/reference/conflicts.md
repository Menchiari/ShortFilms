# Source conflicts and their resolutions

Order of authority: Leonard's beliefs (final say) > McKee (default) > everyone else. A resolution below beats any single rule found elsewhere in the kit. Leonard's beliefs are tested against the evidence first; where the evidence pushes back, that is recorded, but his position stands for his projects.

## A. Conflicts between sources

### 1. Sympathy or empathy
- Template: the hero MUST be sympathetic; use undeserved misfortune or "petting the dog".
- McKee (ch. 7): empathy is required, sympathy optional. Likability is characterization; audiences bond with deep character shown in choices. Macbeth works; Interview with the Vampire's Louis doesn't.
- Resolution: empathy is the requirement. The template's devices are optional tools for creating empathy, never a checklist item. Reason: empathy rests on recognizing shared humanity, which matches Leonard's universality test; likability is local taste.

### 2. Stakes must be death
- Template: the only answer to "what if he fails?" is death, physical if possible, figurative "so strong it might as well be real".
- McKee (ch. 7, 14): the value of a desire is proportional to the risk; the story must reach the end of the line set by its genre and the Negation of the Negation of its value. "Life goes back to normal" is fatal; death is not required.
- Resolution: stakes must be the maximum for this character, and the climax must reach the deepest negative of the story's value. Literal death is one option. A small-looking stake (losing a job, a place on a team, a reputation in a village) passes if the story shows that it destroys what the character's identity rests on. Reason: audiences measure stakes against the character's world, not against an absolute scale (McKee's "human limit established by setting and genre").

### 3. Innocent people must suffer too
- Template: if the hero fails, innocents suffer as well; that is "the stuff of empathy".
- McKee: no such rule; social progression (spreading consequences outward) is one of four ways to express progression, not a requirement.
- Resolution: optional. Use it when the story needs to widen; skip it for inner or personal stories.

### 4. The villain
- Template: a main opponent ruthlessly committed to a mutually exclusive goal; only one winner.
- McKee (ch. 14): forces of antagonism are the sum of everything opposing the protagonist, inner, personal and extra-personal; a villain is one possible form.
- Resolution: agree on strength, differ on form. The opposition must be stronger than the protagonist at the start and reach the Negation of the Negation; it can be a person, an institution, the protagonist himself, or all three. A personal antagonist should want something of his own, not just block.

### 5. Stating the theme
- Template: someone tells the hero "Do you know what your problem is?"; the opposite value of the theme is "clearly stated"; pro and con are voiced by characters; the denouement shows the hero "embracing and embodying" the positive theme.
- McKee (ch. 6, 11, 15): never explain; the climax proves the idea through action; self-explanatory dialogue is phony. Garland: meaning "by inference, not by statement". Anti-slop research: unnecessary exposition is one of the most frequent AI tells.
- Resolution: the thematic argument is dramatized through choices. A character may voice a value only as a weapon in a live conflict, in his own voice, and never as the writer's summary. No character states the theme at the end. Reason: stated meaning removes the audience's insight, which McKee identifies as the reward for paying attention.

### 6. The Hollywood ending
- Template: the hero returns to the ordinary world fully actualized; Private, Personal and Professional conflicts resolve, ideally at once.
- McKee: up, down or ironic; irony lasts longest; the ending must keep the promise made from the first scenes.
- Leonard: something is gained, something is lost.
- Resolution: the ending's charge comes from the story's truth, defaulting to irony (gain and loss stated clearly). Keep the template's useful mechanism: resolving the private, personal and professional conflicts close together multiplies the climax.

### 7. Fixed beat positions
- Template: 12 Act I plot points, 28 Act II reversals; Snyder, Vogler, Harmon and others fix beats at points.
- McKee: principles, not formulas; three major reversals minimum for a feature; act count and placement vary.
- Kaufman: "There's no template for a screenplay, or there shouldn't be."
- Catmull: Disney's 1990s template ("a musical with five to seven songs and a funny sidekick") preceded its decline.
- Resolution: beat models are diagnostic checklists and vocabulary, never a template to fill. Position comes from the runtime math in structure.md; McKee's five parts are the only required elements.

### 8. Breaking structure
- McKee: classical design mirrors how people remember and anticipate; master it first; break it only to put something more important in its place.
- Kaufman: form is not pre-established; "Do not simplify."
- Catmull: "There isn't the recipe"; templates mislead.
- Leonard: break structure to make new things, when you know the reason and the effect.
- Resolution: any break must be declared in writing: which classical element is removed or reversed, where it sits on the story triangle, what the audience loses (for example, the closed answer), what it gains, and what replaces the missing element. If those five lines can't be written, the break is not allowed. See structure.md, "Breaking structure on purpose".

### 9. Outline or discover
- King: situation first, then let the story be found like a fossil; he distrusts plotting.
- Wilbur Smith: knows the ending and the opening, no notes, lets imagination roam.
- Stine: outlines every chapter and its ending before writing.
- McKee: months of step outline before treatment and script. Cameron: scriptments.
- Resolution: for screen work, outline first (screenplays are short, structure-bound, and expensive to fix late), but leave discovery inside the outline: the treatment and draft may change scenes; the climax decides what stays. The writer should be surprised by the story at least once (McKee ch. 6).

### 10. Voice-over
- McKee: telling narration is lazy; keep only what survives the removal test.
- Kubrick: voice-over is "a perfectly legitimate and economical way of conveying story information" in Barry Lyndon, where it announces outcomes in advance.
- Resolution: compatible on inspection. Kubrick's narration creates dramatic irony (we know before the character), which McKee endorses. Allowed: narration that adds irony, counterpoint or economy for a story too large to dramatize. Banned: narration that explains feelings or meaning.

### 11. Constant cliffhangers
- Stine: "every chapter is a cliffhanger". Raiders conference: "each cliffhanger is better than the one before."
- McKee: alternate tension and release; repeated emotion loses force (Law of Diminishing Returns).
- Resolution: end scenes on a turn that opens a question, but vary the size and charge. A cliffhanger is a question, not a peak. Escalate the size of reversals across acts, not every scene.

### 12. Freedom and craft
- Bukowski: "stay loose, work wild and easy and fail any way you want to"; craft became Hemingway's cage.
- Kaufman: craft belongs "in second position".
- McKee: craft frees the subconscious.
- Resolution: by phase. Excavation and first drafts are loose (King's door closed); revision is craft (door open). The Braintrust review checks craft only after a draft exists.

### 13. Writing for yourself or for the audience
- Smith: "I write for myself". King: one Ideal Reader.
- McKee: talented writers fail when they write to prove an idea or vent an emotion; they succeed when they want to move an audience.
- Resolution: Leonard's taste is the first audience; the test is whether a stranger is moved without explanation. Both are required.

### 14. Adaptation
- McKee (ch. 16): "Be willing to reinvent": reorder, cut, create new scenes.
- Garland: "your loyalty is to the film, not the novel".
- Leonard: stay faithful; condensing is fine; adding is not.
- Resolution: Leonard's rule governs. Allowed: cutting, condensing, merging, reordering, and turning described inner states into visible behavior already implied by the source. Not allowed: new events, characters, motives or meanings. If the medium forces a bridge (an inner thought with no visible equivalent), propose it labeled [ADAPTATION BRIDGE] with the source passage it renders, and get approval. Work from the source text, never from memory of it.

### 15. Period settings
- McKee (ch. 4): the past is justified only as a clear glass on the present.
- Leonard: avoid stories that work only because of their era.
- Resolution: the period must supply concrete pressures (what people can and cannot do) while the conflict stays universal. The test is McKee's own archetype rule: universal conflict, culture-specific detail. Topical relevance to today is not required; dependence on knowing the era is forbidden.

## B. Leonard's beliefs tested against the evidence

1. "Every story has a beginning, middle and end; usually it's about overcoming an obstacle."
   - Supported: McKee's five-part design, Aristotle, every model on the Carlson chart.
   - Challenge: "overcoming" implies success. McKee's quest is "he may or may not achieve it"; Punitive and Disillusionment plots end in failure; Miniplot change can be inner. And an obstacle means nothing without desire: the story is the desire, the obstacle is what makes it cost something.
   - Kept as: a beginning (balance broken), a middle (escalating struggle), an end (irreversible change), success optional.
2. "A simple obstacle can set off a chain until the simple goal becomes almost unreachable."
   - Supported: McKee's progressive complications and points of no return; the RPG catalog's A-B-C Quest; Stine and the Raiders conference on escalating cliffhangers.
   - Caution: the chain must escalate in risk and quality, not just in number (McKee ch. 9: a soft middle repeats actions of the same size).
3. "A balance is broken, there is a point of no return, a new balance is reached. Something gained, something lost."
   - Supported: McKee's inciting incident (balance), points of no return, absolute and irreversible climax; his irony chapter; the Augustine and Boston rows on the Carlson chart are four-state versions of the same arc.
   - Challenge: purely positive or purely negative endings exist in great work (McKee's idealist and pessimist ideas). Irony is the default here, not a law.
4. "Art should hit emotionally without cultural scaffolding; durability and universality are the measure."
   - Supported: McKee's archetype vs stereotype; "Stereotypical stories stay at home, archetypal stories travel."
   - Challenge: universality comes through specificity, not by removing it. A story stripped of concrete culture becomes generic. The rule is: no understanding may depend on insider knowledge; detail may be as local as needed.
5. "Adaptations stay faithful."
   - Contradicted by McKee and Garland. Stands for Leonard's projects with the procedure in A.14.
