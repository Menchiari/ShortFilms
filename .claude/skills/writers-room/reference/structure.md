# Structure

Load at step 2 (triangle position), step 5 (beat sheet, scene list) and when a structure break is proposed. Models here are diagnostic vocabulary, not templates (conflicts.md, A.7).

## 1. Required elements (McKee, the only non-negotiable layer)
1. Inciting Incident: on screen; radically upsets the protagonist's balance; creates the conscious desire (and the unconscious one in a complex protagonist); raises the major dramatic question; plants the obligatory scene.
2. Progressive Complications: each failed action is a point of no return; risk, will and capacity required keep rising; conflict on all three levels where possible.
3. Crisis: final dilemma (two goods or two evils) facing the strongest antagonism; static, on screen.
4. Climax: absolute, irreversible change of the central value; clear without explanation; ideally contains one last turn.
5. Resolution: leftovers, spread of effects, slow curtain.
Plus: three major reversals minimum for a feature; the last two act climaxes have opposite charges.

What the alignment shows: every model agrees on four movements: a stable state disturbed; a search under rising pressure; a collapse or ordeal near the end of the middle; a final act that settles the question irreversibly. The theological rows (Augustine, Boston) are the same shape with moral states: integrity, fall, recovery, a new state. That is Leonard's belief #3 in its oldest form. Disagreement is only in detail and naming.

Template notes worth keeping as tools:
- Stakes Character: one person whose fate makes the abstract stake human and who has the quality the hero lacks.
- Deflector: someone who pulls the hero off his path without being the main antagonist (often a well-meaning friend).
- Third-act solution planted in Act II and not understood until Act III (a setup/payoff, McKee ch. 10).
- Central Question in three layers: Professional (everyone sees it), Personal (a few know), Private (alone). Resolving them close together multiplies the climax.
- The Formula (A/B/C/D): "When a [type of person] [has/does/wants/gets] A, he [gets/does/tries/learns] B, only to discover that C now happens; and he must respond by doing D." A = Orphan, B = Wanderer, C = Warrior, D = Martyr. 60 to 80 words. Use it as a stress test: if C and D can't be written, there is no ending yet.

## 3. The story triangle (McKee ch. 2)
- Archplot: active single protagonist, external conflict emphasized, causality, linear time, consistent reality, closed ending.
- Miniplot: open ending, internal conflict emphasized, possibly multiple or passive protagonists; must still change something.
- Antiplot: coincidence over causality, nonlinear time, inconsistent reality.
- Nonplot (outside the triangle): nothing changes.
Place every story with a short line per spectrum: ending (closed/open), conflict (external/internal), protagonist (single/multi, active/passive), time (linear/nonlinear), causality/coincidence, reality (consistent/inconsistent). Then name the corner it leans toward. Audience size and budget follow the position.

## 4. Breaking structure on purpose
Allowed only with a written declaration, kept in the story bible:
1. The element broken (for example: no closed ending; protagonist switch at the midpoint; reverse chronology; inciting incident delayed into the middle; no visible antagonist).
2. The triangle movement it creates (toward Miniplot or Antiplot, and how far).
3. What the audience loses (for example: the answer to the major dramatic question; empathy built through time; curiosity about outcome).
4. What it gains (for example: dramatic irony and dread, as Betrayal's reverse order or Barry Lyndon's announced outcomes; the shock of Psycho's protagonist switch; the empathy Kiss of the Spider Woman protects by delaying its inciting incident).
5. What replaces the missing element (McKee: break convention only "to put something more important in its place").
If any line can't be filled, propose the classical version instead and say so.

Known safe breaks with known effects:
- Reveal the ending first: trades curiosity about outcome for dread and attention to motive (dramatic irony).
- Delay the central plot's inciting incident: requires a setup subplot that carries the opening and must not mislead about genre.
- Open ending: leaves one question answerable within clear limited alternatives; never an unfinished story.
- Multiplot: no spine; unity must come from one controlling idea played in variations.
- Nonlinear time: the audience must still be able to reconstruct order, or the story becomes antiplot.

## 5. Runtime math
Base rates (McKee): one formatted page is about one minute; a two-hour feature has 40 to 60 scenes (average 2 to 3 minutes, varied), 12 to 18 sequences, 3 or more acts. A camera exhausts a single setup in about two to three minutes.

| Format | Pages | Scenes | Sequences | Acts (major reversals) | Inciting incident by | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Micro short (1 to 5 min) | 1 to 5 | 1 to 4 | 1 | 1 | first 20% | one turn; often one scene |
| Short (5 to 20 min) | 5 to 20 | 4 to 12 | 1 to 3 | 1 | first 20% | McKee: one act |
| Medium (20 to 60 min) | 20 to 60 | 10 to 30 | 4 to 9 | 2 | first 15% | sitcom, hour drama, novella scale |
| Feature (85 to 100 min) | 85 to 100 | 35 to 50 | 10 to 14 | 3 to 4 | ~10 to 15 min | Act One climax ~20 to 25 min; last act ~15 min |
| Feature (110 to 130 min) | 110 to 130 | 45 to 60 | 12 to 18 | 3 to 5 | ~10 to 30 min (setup subplot if late) | Act One climax ~25 to 30 min; last act ≤ 20 min |
| Epic (150+ min) | 150+ | 60 to 80 | 18 to 24 | 4 to 7 | first 25% | needs subplots or mid-act climaxes |

Formulas:
- scenes ≈ runtime minutes ÷ 2.5 (range ÷ 3 to ÷ 2)
- sequences ≈ scenes ÷ 3 (2 to 5 scenes each)
- Act One ≈ 25% of runtime; last act ≈ 15 to 20%; resolution 1 to 3 minutes.
- The template's 12 Act I points and 28 Act II reversals assume a 120-minute feature: about one reversal every 2 to 3 minutes of Act II, which matches the scene rate. For shorter work, merge plot points; do not cram them.
- AI-generated production (if the repo's pipeline uses generated shots): estimate shots per scene from the pipeline's own documents; never assume a shot length.

## 6. Beat sheet procedure (step 5)
1. From the bible: runtime, controlling idea, value at stake, climax.
2. Write the climax beat first, then the crisis, then the inciting incident (McKee: work backward).
3. Place act climaxes using the runtime table; check opposite charges for the last two.
4. Fill progressive complications as points of no return; label each with its level (inner, personal, extra-personal) and its position on the negative declension (Contrary, Contradictory, Negation of the Negation).
5. Run the template and the chart as a checklist: what's missing that the story needs? Never add a beat just because a model has it.
6. Scene list: every scene gets value, opening charge, closing charge, turning point (action or revelation), and which setup it plants or pays off. A scene with the same opening and closing charge is cut or merged.
